# Research Brief: Layer 3 — Historic Technology Disruptions & Lessons for the AI Transition

**Project:** AI & The European Job Market — 6-Layer Framework
**Layer:** 3 of 6
**Status:** Research brief (not yet executed)

---

You are a labour economics, economic history, and technology historian. Research the full arc of major technology disruptions across two scales — macro (economy-reshaping paradigm shifts) and micro (occupation-level transformations) — to extract structural patterns that predict how the current AI transition will play out across European labour markets.

---

## PART A — MACRO DISRUPTIONS: Paradigm Shifts That Reshaped Entire Economies

These are General Purpose Technologies (GPTs) that didn't just automate tasks — they restructured what work *is*, where it happens, who does it, and what the economy produces. AI is credibly in this category.

### For each, research:

- What the economy and labour market looked like *before*
- The transition period — how long from invention to widespread economic restructuring?
- What was destroyed (sectors, occupations, social structures, geographies)
- What was created (new sectors, new occupations, new social structures, new geographies)
- The "J-curve" — was there a period where disruption outpaced creation? How long did it last?
- The distributional story — who won, who lost, and was the pain concentrated geographically, demographically, or by class?
- The institutional response — what role did policy, education, unions, and regulation play in shaping outcomes?
- The productivity paradox — was there a lag between technology adoption and measurable productivity gains? (Solow's paradox for computers; similar patterns for electricity and steam)

### Case studies (chronological):

#### 1. Printing press (1440s–1500s)

Gutenberg's press eliminated scribes but created an entire publishing/knowledge economy. Estimated 80% cost reduction in book production. The lag between invention and societal restructuring (Reformation, scientific revolution, mass literacy). What happened to the monasteries that previously held the monopoly on text reproduction? The creation of entirely new occupations: typesetters, publishers, booksellers, journalists. European geographic variation in adoption speed and impact.

#### 2. Mechanised loom / textile industrialisation (1760s–1830s)

The Luddite movement as the canonical "workers vs machines" story. Hand-loom weavers' wages and employment before and after the power loom. The shift from cottage industry to factory system. Did total textile employment grow or shrink? The creation of the industrial working class. The 60+ year transition period. Child labour, urbanisation, public health crises as transition costs. The role of the Combination Acts and eventual legalisation of trade unions.

#### 3. Steam engine / railways (1800s–1870s)

The elimination of coaching inns, canal workers, turnpike operators. The creation of entirely new geographies of economic activity (railway towns). The speed of adoption — UK rail network built in ~30 years. Employment effects: did the displaced canal workers become railway workers? The role of the "railway mania" speculative bubble (1840s) — parallels to AI investment hype cycles?

#### 4. Electricity / electrification (1880s–1930s)

Paul David's research on the 40-year lag between electricity availability and factory productivity gains (factories had to be *redesigned*, not just electrified). The elimination of the "power shaft" factory layout and the occupations tied to it. The creation of entirely new consumer industries (appliances, entertainment, retail). Rural electrification as a geographic equity story. The productivity paradox: why did it take so long?

#### 5. Assembly line / mass production (1910s–1950s)

Ford's $5 day and the creation of the consumer-worker. The elimination of craft-based automobile production. Deskilling vs upskilling debate. The role of unions (UAW) in ensuring displaced craft workers shared in productivity gains. The geographic concentration of manufacturing (Detroit, Midlands, Ruhr).

#### 6. The computer / mainframe era (1950s–1980s)

The elimination of "computer" as a human job title (women who performed calculations). The creation of the IT profession. The typing pool, the ledger clerk, the filing clerk — all gradually displaced. The Solow paradox ("you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics"). The lag between computer adoption and white-collar productivity gains.

#### 7. The personal computer / office automation (1980s–2000s)

Desktop publishing → typesetters/compositors eliminated. Word processing → typing pools eliminated. Spreadsheets → what happened to accountants (spoiler: they grew). The net employment effect of the PC revolution — did it create or destroy more jobs in aggregate? The geographic story: did it matter where you were?

#### 8. The internet / e-commerce (1995–2015)

Retail transformation (but physical retail employment didn't collapse as predicted — it transformed). Travel agents, record stores, video rental — clean kills. But also: the creation of entirely new sectors (digital marketing, e-commerce logistics, platform economy, content creation). The dot-com bust as a hype-cycle correction that didn't stop the underlying transformation. The "last mile" problem — why some internet-exposed occupations survived.

#### 9. Mobile / smartphones / platform economy (2007–2020)

The gig economy as a new labour market structure. The elimination of some intermediary roles (dispatchers, travel agents residual). The creation of new categories (app developers, social media managers, content creators, platform workers). The regulatory response in Europe (Platform Work Directive) vs US laissez-faire.

## PART B — MICRO DISRUPTIONS: Occupation-Level Transformations

These are specific technology → occupation interactions that show the *mechanics* of how jobs transform, absorb, or disappear. Less about reshaping the whole economy, more about what happens inside a specific role.

### For each, research:

- The prediction at the time (what people expected)
- What actually happened to employment numbers (with data, ideally BLS/Eurostat historical series)
- The mechanism — *why* did jobs grow, transform, or disappear?
- The timescale from technology introduction to employment impact
- Who won, who lost, and what determined which side you landed on
- Geographic and distributional effects

### Case studies:

#### 1. ATMs → Bank tellers (1970s–2010s)

Bessen's research. Teller employment *grew* despite 400,000 ATMs. Branch cost reduction → market expansion. Teller role shifted from cash handling to relationship banking. The eventual reversal post-2010 when mobile banking broke the branch expansion model.

#### 2. Spreadsheets → Accountants/bookkeepers (1979–2000s)

VisiCalc/Lotus/Excel. BLS data. The shift from computation to advisory. Market expansion — cheaper accounting meant more businesses could afford it.

#### 3. Containerisation → Dockworkers (1956–1980s)

90%+ workforce reduction. Total-task-bundle automation. No remaining human tasks of the original kind. LSE "Off the Waterfront" data showing 40-year geographic scarring in UK port districts. The ILA's 1960 "share of the machine" deal as a managed transition model.

#### 4. CAD → Technical draftsmen (1985–2005)

Standalone role disappearance. Function absorbed into engineering/architecture. No market expansion effect because the output (drawings) was the same, just produced faster.

#### 5. Telephone switchboard operators (1920s–1980s)

From one of the largest female-employing occupations to near-zero. Full task automation with no remaining human component. The geographic concentration (small-town operators displaced with no local alternatives).

#### 6. Elevator operators (1900s–1960s)

The 50-year adoption curve despite technology being ready much earlier. The 1945 NYC strike. Social/institutional friction as a brake on technology adoption. What this teaches about how fast workplaces actually change even when the technology is mature.

#### 7. Desktop publishing → Typesetters/compositors (1985–2000)

A knowledge-work disruption. The Linotype operator as a high-skill, well-paid role that simply vanished. The speed of the transition (~15 years from DTP introduction to near-complete displacement). What happened to the displaced workers — did they transition to DTP roles or leave the industry?

#### 8. Industrial robots → Manufacturing assembly workers (1960s–present)

The ongoing automation of automotive and electronics assembly. Geographic concentration (Rust Belt, Midlands). The role of unions in managing vs resisting the transition. Japan/Germany vs US adoption curves and labour market outcomes.

#### 9. GPS/routing software → Taxi dispatchers / navigators (2000s–2010s)

A quiet elimination of an entire occupation category. Also: what Uber/Lyft did to the taxi driver role (not elimination but restructuring of the employment relationship).

#### 10. E-commerce → Retail salespeople (2000s–present)

The incomplete disruption. US retail employment actually *grew* through the e-commerce era (until ~2017). The shift from product knowledge to experience/service. The geographic story (malls vs urban retail vs rural).

### PART B analytical output:

Build a **disruption pattern classification:**

- **Type 1 — Partial task automation → Job transformation + market expansion** (ATM pattern)
- **Type 2 — Full task automation → Job elimination** (container pattern)
- **Type 3 — Role absorption** — function survives, standalone role doesn't (CAD pattern)
- **Type 4 — Market restructuring** — the job persists but the employment relationship changes (Uber/taxi pattern)
- **Type 5 — Demand creation** — technology creates entirely new occupations that didn't exist before

**For each type, identify the diagnostic variables that predict which pattern a given occupation will follow:**

- Does the technology automate *some* tasks or *all* core tasks in the bundle?
- Does cost reduction expand the addressable market?
- Are the remaining human tasks high-value or low-value?
- Is there a physical/relational/judgment component the technology cannot replicate?
- How fast does the technology reach maturity vs how fast do institutions adapt?
- Is there union/regulatory friction that slows adoption (and does that help or hurt workers long-term)?

## PART C — CROSS-CUTTING ANALYSIS

### C.1 The acceleration question

Plot the time from invention to mass adoption across all macro disruptions. Is the curve actually accelerating? (Common claim, but David Edgerton and others argue it's more nuanced.) What does the empirical adoption-speed data predict for AI's timeline?

### C.2 The "this time it's different" question

Every GPT has prompted "this time is different" claims. Identify the specific structural arguments for why AI *might* be different from previous GPTs:

- **Breadth:** AI potentially automates cognitive *and* physical tasks (with robotics) simultaneously — no previous GPT did both
- **Speed:** capability improvement measured in months, not decades
- **Generality:** the same technology (transformer/LLM) applies across almost all knowledge work, unlike previous technologies that were sector-specific
- **Capital-labour substitution:** previous GPTs augmented human capability; AI can potentially *replace* the human entirely in some task chains

Then steelman the counter-arguments:

- **Adoption ≠ capability:** enterprises move slowly regardless of what the technology can do
- **Regulation:** EU AI Act, Platform Work Directive, works councils — Europe has institutional friction the US doesn't
- **Tacit knowledge:** the Polanyi paradox — "we know more than we can tell," and AI still struggles with embodied, contextual, relational knowledge
- **Jevons paradox:** cheaper cognitive work → more demand for cognitive work → more jobs, not fewer

### C.3 The European vs US divergence

Previous technology disruptions played out differently in Europe vs the US due to:

- Stronger unions and works councils (especially DACH)
- More robust social safety nets (unemployment insurance, retraining programmes)
- Stricter employment protection legislation (notice periods, severance, unfair dismissal)
- Slower but more managed transitions
- Different sectoral composition (more manufacturing, more public sector)

Research whether European institutional frameworks led to *better* or *worse* outcomes for displaced workers across the historical cases. Did managed transition produce less pain but slower adaptation? Did US flexibility produce more pain but faster reallocation?

### C.4 The "what happened to the displaced workers" question

For each micro disruption, trace what actually happened to the specific humans who lost their jobs:

- Did they retrain into the transformed version of their role?
- Did they move to adjacent occupations?
- Did they leave the labour force entirely?
- Was age the critical variable? (Older workers permanently displaced, younger workers adapted?)
- What role did geography play? (Could they physically access new opportunities?)

This is the least-researched and most important question for the synthesis layer.
