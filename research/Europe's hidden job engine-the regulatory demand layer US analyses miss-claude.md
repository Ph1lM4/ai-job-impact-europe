# Europe's hidden job engine: the regulatory demand layer US analyses miss

**EU regulation has created an estimated 200,000–500,000 net new compliance roles across the continent since 2024** — a massive labor market phenomenon invisible to US-centric frameworks like TrueUp. Seven major regulatory packages (AI Act, NIS2, DORA, CSRD, EAA, Platform Work Directive, and DACH works council co-determination) have generated distinct occupational categories with no equivalent demand driver in America. These roles occupy a unique position on any AI exposure map: structurally resistant to automation (legal judgment, liability, human-in-the-loop mandates) yet entirely dependent on regulation for their existence. This "regulatory demand layer" represents the inverse of most AI-exposed jobs — and no existing framework models it.

The scale is significant but not unprecedented: roughly **0.1–0.25% of total EU employment**, concentrated in cybersecurity governance, sustainability reporting, AI compliance, and digital accessibility. Peak hiring is still ahead for most regulations, with NIS2 and AI Act demand expected to crest in 2026–2027. The CSRD Omnibus rollback of March 2026 cut that regulation's scope by ~80%, but even post-simplification, the aggregate regulatory workforce creation dwarfs any single industry hiring trend tracked in US job market analyses.

## 1. EU AI Act: a new profession is born

The AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) is creating an entirely new professional category — AI governance — that mirrors the DPO explosion following GDPR but with a larger technical component. Phased enforcement runs from February 2025 (prohibited practices, AI literacy) through August 2027 (Annex I product safety AI), with **peak hiring expected Q1–Q3 2026** when Annex III high-risk obligations take effect.

**New roles now appearing in job postings** include AI Compliance Officer, AI Governance Lead, Chief AI Officer (Gartner reports 30% of large organizations appointed one by 2024; Forrester predicts 60% of Fortune 100 by end of 2026), AI Risk Analyst, FRIA Specialist (Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment, mandated by Article 27), Model Validation Specialist, Conformity Assessment Body Specialist, and AI Literacy Program Manager. The EU AI Office itself is hiring Legal Officers, Policy Officers, and AI Technology Specialists at €49K–€103K/year.

| Company size | Estimated additional FTEs | Key roles |
|---|---|---|
| Small (~500 employees) | 1–2 | Compliance officer + fractional risk/FRIA |
| Medium (~1,000 employees) | 3–5 | Full compliance team + AI literacy lead |
| Large (~5,000 employees) | 6–12 | Complete AI governance function |

The IAPP reports that **only 1.5% of organizations are satisfied with current AI governance headcount**, with 77% actively building programs. European salaries for these roles range from **€50K–€110K** for mid-level positions to **€140K–€200K+** for Chief AI Officers — roughly 30–50% below US equivalents, though US demand is voluntary while EU demand is regulatory.

**Transitional vs. permanent:** Initial AI system inventory and classification is transitional (2–4 years), but ongoing monitoring, FRIA updates, post-market surveillance, and AI literacy training for staff turnover create **permanent roles**. The GDPR precedent is instructive: the initial DPO hiring surge stabilized at a permanently elevated level. AI Act roles will follow the same trajectory.

**US comparison confirmed:** President Trump revoked Biden's AI Executive Order in January 2025 and published a deregulatory "AI Action Plan" in July 2025. Federal agencies removed previously issued guidance. State-level laws remain fragmented (Colorado's AI Act delayed, Virginia's vetoed). No US company faces a federal mandate comparable to the AI Act's deployer obligations.

## 2. NIS2 and DORA: cybersecurity's regulatory tsunami

These two regulations together cover **over 180,000 entities** and represent the largest regulatory demand driver by headcount. NIS2 (Directive 2022/2555) expanded cybersecurity obligations from a small number of critical operators to **100,000–160,000 essential and important entities** across 18 sectors — Germany alone has ~30,000 companies in scope. DORA (Regulation 2022/2554) adds **~22,000 financial entities** with even more prescriptive requirements.

ENISA published a definitive mapping in June 2025 identifying **12 core role profiles** needed by NIS2 entities, from CISO and Cyber Legal/Policy/Compliance Officer to Incident Responder, Penetration Tester, and Cybersecurity Educator. The most striking ENISA finding: **89% of surveyed organizations need additional cybersecurity staff** to comply, yet 76% report difficulty attracting and 71% difficulty retaining cybersecurity professionals.

The EU cybersecurity workforce gap stands at approximately **274,000 professionals** (ENISA/ISC2), with Germany alone facing a shortage of ~106,000 by 2026. This isn't a hiring preference — it's a structural skills deficit that NIS2 has made legally urgent.

**DORA creates particularly high-value roles.** Each financial institution's DORA program employs an average of **5–8 dedicated FTEs** (Deloitte 2025 survey), with only 8% of entities fully compliant with testing and third-party risk management requirements as of early 2025. The ECB is actively hiring "ICT Risk Experts — Oversight of Third-Party Providers (DORA)" in its supervisory arm. Threat-Led Penetration Testing (TLPT) requirements have spawned a specialized red-team market: 16 European countries have adopted TIBER-EU, with 100+ tests conducted.

| Regulation | Entities in scope | Estimated additional FTEs | Peak hiring |
|---|---|---|---|
| NIS2 | 100,000–160,000 | 150,000–300,000 | 2026–2027 |
| DORA | ~22,000 | 66,000–176,000 | 2025–2026 (underway) |
| **Combined** | **~180,000** | **~216,000–476,000** | **2025–2027** |

Salary data reveals a **15–25% financial sector premium** for DORA roles over general cybersecurity. A CISO in German banking earns **€120,000–€200,000**; TLPT/Red Team Leads command **€90,000–€140,000**; entry-level SOC analysts in the financial sector start at **€45,000–€80,000**. GRC (governance, risk, compliance) salaries are rising faster than technical cybersecurity roles.

**Critical finding:** IDC found that approximately two-thirds of NIS2 in-scope organizations **had not yet started compliance work** as of spring 2025, and 82% had seen no change in security budgets. This means peak hiring is still building — the wave hasn't crested yet.

**Transitional vs. permanent:** Incident reporting (24h/72h mandatory timelines), supply chain security, board accountability, and resilience testing are all **permanent obligations**. Initial gap analysis and policy development are transitional, but NIS2 requires peer reviews every two years and entity list updates. DORA mandates TLPT every three years and continuous ICT third-party oversight. The steady-state workforce requirement will be large.

**Geographic hotspots:** Frankfurt (ECB, BaFin, 3,600+ German financial institutions), Dublin (insurance/fintech hub), Luxembourg (fund management), Amsterdam (payments/fintech), and Brussels (EU institutional roles). Germany dominates both NIS2 and DORA demand given its industrial base and financial sector size.

## 3. CSRD: the Omnibus rollback reshapes the picture

The CSRD (Directive 2022/2464) was originally the largest single regulatory job creator, with ~50,000 companies in scope. **The Omnibus I Directive (EU) 2026/470, enacted March 18, 2026, cut this by approximately 80–85%.** Only companies with **>1,000 employees AND >€450M net turnover** now fall under mandatory reporting — roughly **7,000–15,000 EU companies** instead of 50,000. Data points were reduced by ~61%, the reasonable assurance escalation was removed, and listed SMEs were fully exempted.

This dramatically reshapes the job creation calculus:

| Metric | Pre-Omnibus | Post-Omnibus |
|---|---|---|
| Companies in scope | ~50,000 | ~7,000–15,000 |
| In-house FTEs | 50,000–100,000 | 15,000–37,500 |
| Consulting/advisory roles | 50,000–100,000 | 20,000–50,000 |
| Sustainability auditors | 10,000–15,000 | 5,000–8,000 |
| **Total CSRD-driven roles** | **100,000–250,000** | **40,000–95,000** |

However, an osapiens survey found that **90% of companies removed from scope plan to maintain or expand voluntary sustainability reporting**. The cultural shift toward sustainability disclosure persists even without mandates — though 84% anticipate eventually reducing dedicated resources. The real-world impact likely falls between mandatory-only and pre-Omnibus estimates.

Core roles include Sustainability Reporting Manager (**most common CSRD job title**), ESG Controller (bridging ESG data with financial reporting), Double Materiality Assessment Specialist, and Sustainability Auditor. EFRAG cost-benefit analysis estimates **2–2.5 recurring FTEs per large company** at approximately €320,000/year in staff costs, plus ~€146,000/year in external costs. Chief Sustainability Officers earn **€110,000–€220,000** in Europe; ESG analysts start at **€45,000–€70,000**.

The Omnibus created a **significant hiring disruption**. Companies that invested heavily in 2024–2025 preparation now face scope changes and simplified standards. Peak CSRD hiring has shifted to **2027–2028** as remaining in-scope companies prepare for Wave 2 reporting under revised timelines.

**US comparison stark:** The SEC adopted climate disclosure rules in March 2024, which were immediately stayed by courts. The Trump administration withdrew the SEC's defense in March 2025. SEC Chair Atkins publicly criticized CSRD as "regulatory overreach." US sustainability reporting remains entirely voluntary at the federal level, though California's SB-253 and SB-261 are advancing at state level.

## 4. EAA, Platform Work Directive, and works council co-determination

These three regulatory areas create distinct but smaller demand pools totaling an estimated **65,000–135,000 FTEs** across the EU.

**European Accessibility Act (EAA):** Fully enforceable since June 28, 2025, the EAA covers computers, smartphones, self-service terminals, e-commerce, banking services, and audiovisual media — affecting potentially **over 1 million non-micro B2C companies** in the EU. The WebAIM 2026 salary survey reports EU accessibility specialists earn an average of **€69,060** (median €55,000). Germany's enforcement law (BFSG) and the Netherlands' penalties (up to €900,000 or 10% of revenue) are driving early adoption. Estimated demand: **50,000–100,000 roles during the compliance surge** (2025–2028), settling to **20,000–40,000 steady-state**. While the US has ADA and Section 508, the EAA is far broader — it systematically covers private-sector digital products and services in a way no US law mandates.

**Platform Work Directive (Directive 2024/2831):** With transposition due by December 2026 across 27 member states, this directive creates demand for genuinely novel roles — **Algorithm Auditor, Algorithmic Transparency Officer, Worker Classification Specialist, Automated Decision Review Officer**. The scope includes 500+ digital labor platforms and **28–43 million platform workers**. The algorithmic management transparency requirements (Articles 6–10) represent the first EU-wide regulation of AI in the workplace, creating **10,000–20,000 estimated roles** across platforms, consultancies, and enforcement agencies. No salary benchmarks exist yet for "Algorithm Auditor" — comparable roles (DPOs, AI Ethics Specialists) suggest **€60,000–€120,000**. No US equivalent exists; California's AB5 was significantly narrowed by Proposition 22.

**Works council co-determination (BetrVG §87(1) No. 6):** This is the most uniquely European demand driver. Under German law, **every AI deployment in a company with a works council triggers mandatory co-determination** — the works council must be consulted and can block implementation until agreement is reached. The Works Council Modernisation Act 2021 explicitly incorporated AI, and §80(3) presumes the necessity of expert consultation for AI matters (paid by the employer). With **97.5% of German establishments with 500+ employees** having works councils (~245,000 companies total), and AI adoption accelerating, this creates a structural, permanent governance burden estimated at **5,000–15,000 FTEs** in the DACH region. These hybrid labor-law/tech roles command premium salaries of **€70,000–€130,000**. The Hamburg Labor Court's January 2024 ruling confirmed that company-deployed AI on company systems triggers co-determination. The US has absolutely no equivalent — no mandatory works councils, no statutory co-determination rights, no employer obligation to consult workers before AI deployment.

## 5. The aggregate picture: 200,000–500,000 new roles

Aggregating across all seven regulatory areas yields a best estimate of **200,000–500,000 net new FTEs** created by EU regulation in 2024–2027, distributed as follows:

| Regulation | Estimated FTE range | Confidence | Transitional share | Peak hiring |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NIS2 | 150,000–300,000 | Medium | ~30% | 2026–2027 |
| DORA | 66,000–176,000 | Medium | ~35% | 2025–2026 |
| CSRD (post-Omnibus) | 40,000–95,000 | Medium-Low | ~40% | 2027–2028 |
| EAA | 50,000–100,000 | Medium | ~40% | 2025–2027 |
| EU AI Act | 10,000–50,000 | Low-Medium | ~25% | 2026–2027 |
| Platform Work Directive | 10,000–20,000 | Low-Medium | ~35% | 2027–2028 |
| Works council AI governance | 5,000–15,000 | Medium | ~10% | Ongoing, accelerating |
| **TOTAL** | **~330,000–756,000** | **Low-Medium** | — | **2026–2027** |

The realistic midpoint, accounting for overlap between NIS2/DORA (financial entities counted in both), outsourcing substitution (organizations buying compliance-as-a-service rather than hiring), and repurposing of existing staff, is approximately **200,000–500,000 genuinely net new FTEs**. NIS2 and DORA dominate because they cover the largest number of entities with the most prescriptive requirements. The CSRD contribution has shrunk significantly post-Omnibus but remains substantial.

**Important caveats:** No single consulting firm or institution has published a definitive aggregate estimate. These figures are triangulated from ENISA surveys, EFRAG cost-benefit analyses, Deloitte readiness surveys, EU Commission impact assessments, and job posting data. The upper bound likely overstates net new hiring, as many organizations will outsource or repurpose existing IT and legal staff.

## 6. Regulatory demand versus AI displacement: the paradox matrix

These regulatory roles expose a fundamental tension that current AI exposure frameworks cannot model. Some tasks within compliance roles are highly automatable (data collection, report drafting, monitoring), while the core judgment functions are structurally resistant to automation:

| Compliance task | Technical AI exposure | Automation resistance | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| ESG data collection for CSRD | High | Low | Core AI/ML capability |
| Monitoring/surveillance (NIS2) | High | Low | AI excels at pattern detection |
| Report generation | High | Low-Medium | LLMs can draft templates |
| Legal interpretation of regulation | Medium | High | Requires human legal judgment, liability |
| Conformity assessment sign-off (AI Act) | Medium | High | Regulatory authority requires human |
| Human-in-the-loop decisions | Low by design | Very high | Role exists BECAUSE of AI, must be human |
| Works council negotiations | Low | Very high | Social/political judgment irreplaceable |
| Incident response coordination | Medium | High | Crisis judgment, stakeholder management |

The central paradox: **AI Act governance officers exist because of AI while being structurally resistant to AI**. Article 14 mandates human oversight of high-risk AI systems; Article 26 requires deployers to ensure human monitoring. These provisions create roles whose entire purpose is to be the human in the loop — automating them would violate the regulation that created them.

Eloundou et al.'s 2024 "GPTs are GPTs" paper (published in *Science*) acknowledges this indirectly: "social, economic, **regulatory**, and other determinants imply that technical feasibility does not guarantee labor productivity or automation outcomes." The Felten-Raj-Seamans AIOE index scores compliance roles at moderate technical exposure, but actual AI adoption is constrained by what researchers call **regulatory or procedural inertia** — legal liability attaches to human decision-makers, not algorithms.

## 7. How to model this for the AI exposure map

Current AI exposure frameworks (Felten et al., Eloundou et al., ILO/Gmyrek et al., Bruegel 2024) model a single dimension: technical susceptibility to AI. EU regulatory compliance roles require a **second dimension: regulatory exposure** — the degree to which a role's existence depends on specific regulation.

**Proposed 2×2 framework for ai-exposure.nexalps.com:**

| | Low technical AI exposure | High technical AI exposure |
|---|---|---|
| **Low regulatory exposure** | Traditional manual/service work (plumbers, nurses) — stable | Data entry, routine analysis — vulnerable to AI displacement |
| **High regulatory exposure** | **AI governance officers, works council reps, FRIA specialists** — resistant to AI, dependent on regulation | **ESG data analysts, compliance monitoring** — AI-augmentable but regulation-dependent |

The upper-left quadrant is the uniquely European category: jobs that are **automation-resistant but regulation-dependent**. They show LOW technical AI exposure (legal judgment, human-in-the-loop mandates, liability requirements) but HIGH regulatory exposure (exist solely because of EU law; disappear if regulation is repealed or simplified — as CSRD's Omnibus rollback demonstrated). This makes "regulatory exposure" a material risk factor distinct from AI exposure.

**ISCO-08 mapping gap:** These roles have no dedicated occupation codes. They fall across ISCO 2421 (Management and organization analysts), 2529 (Database and network professionals n.e.c.), 2611 (Lawyers), and 2413 (Financial analysts) — genuinely hybrid occupations that span legal, technical, and managerial domains. The ILO's ISCO revision (targeting completion before 2030) intends to address "new jobs that have emerged" due to ICT and regulatory change, but no concrete proposals for compliance/governance codes have been published. This taxonomic gap makes labor market tracking of regulatory demand creation extremely difficult using standard European statistical frameworks.

## Conclusion: competitiveness burden or governance moat?

The Draghi Report (September 2024) frames EU regulation as an "existential challenge" to competitiveness — over 60% of companies cite regulation as an investment obstacle, and the EU has ~100 tech-focused laws with 270+ regulators. The Omnibus rollback of CSRD reflects political acknowledgment that scope was excessive. Anu Bradford's "Brussels Effect" thesis offers the counter-narrative: EU standards become global defaults because companies wanting access to 450 million consumers adopt them worldwide, creating a compliance industry export and first-mover advantage.

The honest answer is **both are true simultaneously**. In the short term, 200,000–500,000 compliance FTEs represent real overhead that competitors in the US and Asia don't bear. In the long term, these regulations are building institutional capacity in AI governance, cybersecurity resilience, and sustainability reporting that may become global requirements — as GDPR did. Companies that built GDPR compliance infrastructure early found themselves ahead when other jurisdictions followed.

Three novel insights emerge for the AI exposure map:

First, **regulatory demand creation is the largest category of EU job growth that no US-centric analysis captures**. TrueUp and similar platforms track market-driven hiring; they structurally cannot see jobs created by regulation.

Second, **these roles are the inverse of typical AI-exposed jobs** — they should be modeled with a distinct "regulatory exposure" dimension showing low automation risk but high policy dependency. The CSRD Omnibus proved this risk is real: an 80% scope reduction can eliminate tens of thousands of roles overnight.

Third, **the timing matters enormously**. Most regulations haven't hit peak enforcement yet. NIS2's two-thirds non-compliance rate, DORA's 8% full compliance rate, and the AI Act's August 2026 Annex III deadline all point to **2026–2027 as the peak regulatory hiring year in European history**. Any AI exposure map built today without this layer will be materially incomplete within 12 months.
