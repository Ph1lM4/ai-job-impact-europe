# **The State of Software and AI Engineering Hiring in Europe (2025–2026)**

## **Executive Summary and Macro Market Baseline**

The European technology labor market in the 2025–2026 cycle is undergoing a period of profound structural transformation, defined by the rapid operationalization of artificial intelligence, tightening macroeconomic conditions, and an accelerating demographic cliff. The empirical data indicates a stark bifurcation within the engineering workforce: there is a state of hyper-demand for specialized, senior-level "deep tech" talent—particularly in Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML), and cybersecurity—contrasted with a precipitous collapse in entry-level hiring.1

To contextualize the European landscape, it is necessary to benchmark against global demand indicators. Lenny’s late-2025 to early-2026 global baseline established a total of over 67,000 open technology roles globally, with approximately 26,000 concentrated in the United States, alongside a noted acceleration in demand since January 2026\. While this baseline suggests a stabilizing and accelerating global market, the European theater operates on a vastly different scale of necessity and demographic constraint. Europe currently employs over 10 million Information and Communication Technology (ICT) specialists, representing roughly 5.1% of total employment across the continent.4 However, to meet the European Union's ambitious "Digital Decade" target of 20 million digital experts by 2030, the continent faces an unprecedented mandate to recruit nearly 10 million additional tech workers within an increasingly constrained timeframe.4 Currently, 57% of EU firms report an inability to find qualified technical staff, indicating that the supply-demand mismatch is already acute.4

This exhaustive research report dissects the European software and AI engineering hiring landscape for 2025–2026. It analyzes the specific volumes of open roles across top markets, the volatile dynamics of the AI salary premium, the alarming contraction of the junior talent pipeline, geographic arbitrage through Eastern European nearshoring, and the profound shifts in organizational design as founders rebuild their engineering organizations around AI-native workflows.

## **Total Open Engineering Roles by European Country**

The European tech job market reached over €150 billion in combined revenue from top tech firms by the end of 2025, operating within an ecosystem valued at nearly $4 trillion.4 Despite venture capital funding gaps compared to the United States—$62 billion in Europe versus $209 billion in the US in 2024—Europe generated more high-tech startups than the US annually between 2019 and 2024, driving relentless, localized demand for software engineers.6

The demand is not distributed evenly. Western and Northern Europe remain the primary hubs for high-value architectural and AI engineering roles, while Central and Eastern Europe (CEE) are rapidly absorbing implementation and operational demands. The baseline job vacancy rate in the broader EU stands at 2.1%, though key hubs like the Netherlands (4.2%), Belgium (3.9%), and Austria (3.4%) exhibit significantly tighter labor markets.7 Overall hiring rates in European tech in 2025-2026 have declined by a marginal 1.4%, stabilizing at 28.6% across markets, yet individual countries present highly divergent trajectories.2

## **AI and Machine Learning Engineering: Volume, Growth Rate, and Total Openings**

While general software engineering hiring has stabilized, the sub-surface dynamics reveal an explosive pivot toward Artificial Intelligence. Organizations have definitively transitioned from the 2024-2025 hype cycle of generative experimentation into a phase of deep integration, requiring specialists capable of deploying, scaling, and maintaining AI systems in live production environments.1

The volume, growth metrics, and market penetration for AI/ML roles in 2026 indicate a fundamental restructuring of the tech workforce. Job titles explicitly containing "AI" have grown by an unprecedented 578% year-over-year, scaling from a mere 0.32% of all tech roles in early 2024 to 2.17% of total job postings in 2025/2026.3 When expanding the criteria beyond explicit titles to encompass required skill sets, the penetration is far deeper. AI-related skills are now explicitly required in approximately 25% of all technology job listings across the broader market, and this figure rises to over 33% within the core information sector.20

In terms of direct hiring volume, core AI and Machine Learning engineering functions have grown 88% year-over-year across all European markets.2 Looking forward, the demand for pure Machine Learning engineers specifically is projected to grow by an astonishing 383% by the end of 2026, vastly outpacing any other technical discipline.4

This surge is not merely aspirational; it reflects immediate operational necessity. "AI/Machine Learning expertise" currently ranks as the absolute top priority skill across European technology leaders. Among enterprise organizations with more than 1,000 employees, 100% of surveyed respondents listed AI engineering as a top hiring priority for 2025 and 2026\.3 The nature of the roles has also shifted. The market is no longer seeking generalists who can interface with third-party APIs via basic prompt engineering. Instead, the hyper-demand is focused on MLOps engineers who can architect complex model deployment pipelines, AI safety specialists navigating the stringent requirements of the EU AI Act, and developers capable of optimizing Large Language Model (LLM) performance on proprietary enterprise data.4

## **The AI Salary Premium Over General Software Engineering**

The intense competition for a limited pool of highly qualified AI professionals has drastically distorted traditional compensation frameworks across Europe. Base salaries for general software engineering are expected to see a stable, moderate rise of 3% to 5% in 2025-2026, reflective of cooling inflation and macroeconomic stabilization.2 However, AI capabilities command substantial and highly disruptive premiums.

Data from the Ravio 2026 Compensation Trends report provides a critical baseline: AI/ML roles command an average base salary premium of 12% at the Professional (Individual Contributor \- IC) level across Europe.2 Interestingly, Ravio notes that this premium drops to just 3% at the Management level.2 This inversion of traditional hierarchical compensation models suggests a highly tactical market. Companies are willing to pay a premium for hands-on, deep-tech contributors who directly build, integrate, and deploy AI models, rather than for the managers who oversee them, recognizing that the bottleneck lies in execution, not administration.21

However, broader staffing benchmarks, such as those from Index.dev and specialized AI staffing agencies, report total compensation premiums ranging from 20% to 40% for niche AI/ML specializations, particularly for AI Architects and Senior ML Engineers.1 This discrepancy between Ravio's 12% base premium and the 40% total premium highlights the evolving nature of "Total Rewards" in 2026\. Because increasing base salaries permanently breaks internal HR payroll structures and creates extreme pay equity issues with existing staff, companies are heavily leveraging alternative compensation levers. Therefore, while the base salary premium averages 12%, the total compensation premium is pushed to 30-40% through enhanced equity grants, massive sign-on bonuses, and unique technical perks.22 For example, 72% of AI professionals now evaluate job offers based on access to high-performance GPU clusters or specialized compute credits, effectively treating computational infrastructure as a form of untaxed compensation.1

## **Junior and Entry-Level Engineering: A 73% Structural Decline**

Perhaps the most alarming metric to emerge from the 2025-2026 European tech labor data is the systemic collapse of entry-level hiring. According to Ravio's benchmarking, hiring rates for entry-level (P1 and P2) positions plummeted by a staggering 73.4% overall from 2024 to early 2025\.3 Within the engineering function specifically, entry-level hiring dropped by 72.2%.3 While the overall market saw a mild 7% decrease in hiring rates, the bottom of the talent funnel has essentially disappeared.

This is not a cyclical dip; the evidence suggests a permanent, structural eradication of traditional junior roles. The primary driver of this sharp decline correlates directly with the operationalization of AI coding assistants and autonomous agents. Tasks traditionally assigned to junior developers—such as writing boilerplate code, basic unit testing, code translation, and routine debugging—are now executed in seconds by mid-level and senior engineers utilizing AI copilots.3

The question of whether this 73% decline is consistent across European countries reveals nuanced regional variations in how the collapse is manifesting, though the ultimate outcome remains identical 23:

* **Germany:** The market maintains strong apprenticeship models in traditional trades, but lacks a scalable equivalent for the technology sector. Consequently, junior tech talent is entirely bottlenecked without a structured pathway into enterprise roles.23  
* **France:** Government-sponsored training programs and bootcamps are producing graduates, but they often fall short of connecting this raw talent to real enterprise jobs that now demand AI fluency over basic coding syntax.23  
* **United Kingdom:** Persistent inflation has severely limited startup hiring budgets, forcing companies to allocate their constrained capital exclusively toward senior hires who guarantee immediate return on investment.23  
* **Scandinavia:** The issue is heavily masked by title inflation. Junior positions are simply being rebranded, yet they carry mid-level expectations, requiring 3 to 5 years of experience for roles that once trained raw talent.23

Furthermore, Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) and AI-driven recruitment systems are exacerbating the crisis by automatically scanning CVs for keywords and filtering out applicants without prior commercial experience before they are even reviewed by a human.23

Founders and engineering leaders are creating a deferred crisis. By aggressively cutting P1 and P2 hiring to leverage immediate AI productivity gains, the European tech ecosystem is severing the very training pipeline that produces mid-level and senior engineers. Analysts warn that founders making these hiring decisions today are creating problems they will not feel for another three to five years, at which point the mid-level talent gap will be impossible to fill at any price.23

## **University CS Graduate Output vs. Market Demand**

A critical bottleneck in the European hiring landscape is the fundamental mismatch between the output of higher education institutions and the accelerating, highly specialized demands of the market. Industry data frequently cites a severe global imbalance to benchmark the crisis: traditional university pipelines produce approximately 65,000 computer science graduates annually, against an immediate market demand for 180,000 AI-capable engineers. This results in an expanding 115,000-person deficit that grows wider daily.26

While the 65K versus 180K metric frequently serves as the global baseline (heavily weighted by US output), the European equivalent is similarly disproportionate and constrained by unique demographic factors.

In the United Kingdom, recent data indicates approximately 54,000 students enrolled in computer science programs annually, alongside roughly 7,000 graduates specifically in cybersecurity disciplines.25 Despite a 20% growth in cyber graduates, the UK still faces a persistent workforce gap, heavily relying on non-EU and non-UK students—who now comprise an astonishing 67% of all postgraduate cyber students—to fill higher-level engineering roles.25 The UK estimates that only about 6,000 graduates actually flow into the active cyber security labor market each year, vastly outpaced by employer demand.25

Across the broader European Union, the talent gap is highly quantifiable. A comprehensive report from the European Chips Skills Academy (ECSA) projected an initial shortfall of 75,400 skilled hardware and software engineers by 2030\. Even after adjusting for localized economic downturns and delayed infrastructure projects in 2024 and 2025, the revised deficit remains at a critical 65,000 highly skilled engineering positions across the EU27.27 This corresponds to an average annual shortfall of roughly 10,800 specialized workers that the university system simply cannot produce.27

Compounding the educational shortfall is "The Great Developer Retirement Wave." In Germany, which currently faces an open IT vacancy pool of over 109,000 positions, the aging workforce represents an existential threat to digital infrastructure.9 Across the continent, baby boomers occupying senior architectural and principal engineering roles are retiring, taking decades of institutional knowledge with them. Bureau of Labor Statistics and European equivalents show that 18% of senior developers plan to retire before 2027\.26 Replacing one retiring principal engineer often requires hiring two senior developers to cover the knowledge gap—a demographic math equation that current university outputs cannot solve.26 Consequently, countries like Germany are aggressively reforming immigration laws, such as the EU Blue Card updates, to import technical talent, particularly targeting India and other non-EU markets to bypass the domestic graduate bottleneck.9

## **Nearshoring Trends and Geographic Arbitrage**

Faced with severe domestic talent shortages and the exorbitant costs of hiring senior AI talent in tier-one cities like London, Zurich, or Munich, European tech companies are pivoting aggressively toward nearshoring in Central and Eastern Europe (CEE).4 This is no longer traditional, low-cost offshore outsourcing focused on basic QA or maintenance; it is defined by analysts as "high-end talent scouting".4 Companies are establishing sophisticated R\&D hubs in the CEE region, deploying a hybrid talent model that pairs Western European architects with elite Eastern European implementation teams.12

The primary Eastern European countries absorbing this overflow demand include:

* **Poland (The Scalable Anchor)**: With a massive talent pool of roughly 300,000 developers, Poland is the primary destination for nearshoring. Cities like Warsaw, Krakow, and Wroclaw operate as mature enterprise and cloud migration hubs, absorbing complex DevOps and cybersecurity functions.4  
* **Romania (The Stealth Wealth Hub)**: Cities like Bucharest and Cluj have matured into premium product development centers. Romanian engineers specialize heavily in blockchain, JavaScript architectures, and AI integration.12  
* **Serbia (The Dark Horse)**: Belgrade has emerged as the premier destination for extreme geographic arbitrage. Serbia offers a highly skilled talent pool with gross salaries significantly lower than Western Europe. Because of favorable flat-tax regimes and low living costs, the net savings and purchasing power for engineers are incredibly high, making it a magnet for remote workers and corporate outposts alike.18

The financial logic driving this nearshoring trend is undeniable when evaluating the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO). In Germany, employers pay approximately 21% to 22% on top of gross salaries for mandatory pension, health, and unemployment insurance.19 When factoring in these social security contributions, office infrastructure (which costs €2,000 to €4,200 per year per workstation in Munich or Frankfurt), and recruiting delays, the costs become prohibitive.19

## **Founders' Expectations and Organizational AI Headcount**

The integration of Generative AI is fundamentally rewriting the organizational chart of the European engineering department. Founders, CEOs, and CTOs are rapidly recalibrating their expectations for how software is built, leading to shifting ratios of human engineers to AI systems over the next 12 to 18 months.

Gartner projects that 80% of the entire engineering workforce will require significant AI upskilling by 2027 as generative AI spawns entirely new roles in software engineering and operations.30 Currently, 45% to 55% of enterprises are already mandating the use of GenAI copilots for code generation, review, and automated testing.31 This has shifted the baseline of productivity. In the short term, AI tools generate modest productivity increases, but by the medium term (late 2026), the rise of autonomous AI agents is expected to fully automate routine developer work patterns, marking a transition toward "AI-native software engineering" where the vast majority of boilerplate code will be AI-generated.30

When founders look 12 months ahead, expectations regarding engineering headcount diverge based on adoption maturity, revealing a complex paradox:

* **The Efficiency Contraction**: Approximately 33% of business leaders explicitly state they plan to use AI to reduce human headcount over the next 18 months, utilizing AI as a direct labor replacement for junior developers and administrative staff.32 As noted earlier, 38% of organizations have already proactively reduced entry-level hiring in anticipation of these AI capabilities.32  
* **The AI Expansion**: Conversely, 45% of leaders plan to maintain their current headcount, deploying AI as "digital labor" to work alongside humans to multiply output. Furthermore, a highly optimistic 67% of CEOs predict an ultimate *increase* in hiring as AI creates entirely new workflows, products, and revenue streams that require human oversight.32

Within the next 12 months, founders expect that pure "AI Engineers"—those operating at the complex intersection of data science, machine learning, and traditional software engineering—will transition from a niche capability to representing a substantial core of the engineering organization. With 56% of software engineering leaders explicitly identifying AI/ML as their most critical skills gap, founders expect the proportion of dedicated AI engineers to scale aggressively, shifting engineering departments from full-stack heavy to AI-infrastructure heavy.30

However, a significant friction point remains at the individual contributor level: the trust paradox. A 2026 survey revealed that while 86% of engineers now use AI tools in their daily workflows, only 6% trust the AI-generated results without hesitation.34 In critical engineering sectors where errors carry legal or financial liability, 89% of engineers still manually verify AI outputs using textbooks, industry manuals, or back-of-the-envelope calculations.35 Founders expecting immediate, flawless automation are encountering the reality that AI currently functions as an accelerant to human review, not a replacement for senior engineering judgment. The human-in-the-loop remains the ultimate bottleneck.

## **Remote Engineering Hiring Trends: Europe vs. The US**

The battle over work location has largely stabilized by 2026, but distinct cultural, regulatory, and operational differences have emerged between the European and United States tech markets regarding remote engineering.

Across advanced economies globally, hybrid work is no longer viewed as a corporate perk; it is the dominant, default operating model. Approximately 83% of workers in remote-capable roles demand some form of hybrid flexibility, and companies failing to offer it face severe retention crises.36

In the United States, the aggressive return-to-office (RTO) mandates pushed heavily by major tech conglomerates and financial institutions in 2024 and 2025 have resulted in a rigid stabilization. As of 2026, 52% of remote-capable employees in the US operate in a strict hybrid model (requiring mandated office days), while only 27% are fully remote, and the remainder are strictly on-site.36 Furthermore, US engineers face a documented and statistically significant "proximity bias." Remote workers in the US are 24% less likely to receive promotions than their on-site peers, and some studies estimate that remote workers earn up to $22,000 less annually than their fully on-site counterparts, penalizing those who opt out of office politics.37

In stark contrast, Europe has embraced remote and hybrid structures much more deeply, utilizing them heavily as a necessary tool for talent acquisition and geographic arbitrage. In the UK, 73% of tech roles explicitly offer hybrid or fully remote options, recognizing that limiting talent pools to a commutable radius is an operational impossibility.38

Because European companies face severe cross-border talent shortages—exemplified by the 109,000 deficit in Germany—they are fundamentally required to hire remotely across the EU and Eastern Europe to survive.9 Global AI teams operating across European time zones have adopted remote and hybrid models as their standard, unquestioned practice.1 Furthermore, with average remote developer salaries in Europe reaching a highly competitive $118,207, the European market is functioning increasingly as a single, distributed, borderless talent pool rather than a collection of isolated national hubs.4 Remote work in Europe is not a lifestyle concession; it is a demographic and economic necessity to access the limited pool of 180,000 required AI engineers against the meager output of 65,000 graduates.
