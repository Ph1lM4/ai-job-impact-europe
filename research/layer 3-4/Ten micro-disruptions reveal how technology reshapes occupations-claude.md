# Ten micro-disruptions reveal how technology reshapes occupations

**Technology does not simply "destroy" or "create" jobs — it triggers one of five distinct disruption patterns, each governed by identifiable structural variables.** Analysis of ten historical technology→occupation interactions spanning 1920–2026 reveals that whether an occupation grows, transforms, or vanishes depends primarily on three factors: the completeness of task automation, the price elasticity of the output market, and the speed of institutional adaptation relative to technological maturation. This taxonomy provides a reusable diagnostic framework for predicting how AI will reshape European labour markets.

The central finding across all ten cases is that **timescale consistently exceeds prediction**. Even the fastest disruptions (desktop publishing, ride-hailing) took 10–15 years; the slowest (elevator operators, telephone switchboards) took 50–90 years. Institutional friction — unions, regulations, public trust — is not merely noise; it is a structural variable that determines whether disruption is managed or catastrophic.

## The ten disruptions: case-by-case evidence

### 1. ATMs → Bank tellers (1970s–2010s) — Type 1: Market expansion

**Prediction:** ATMs would eliminate tellers. President Obama articulated this consensus in 2011: "You use an ATM, you don't go to a bank teller."

**What happened:** Teller employment **doubled** from ~268,000 (1970) to ~608,000 (2007) despite deployment of ~400,000 ATMs, then fell **43%** to 340,820 by 2023 (BLS OEWS, SOC 43-3071). The story has two acts.

**Mechanism:** James Bessen's research (*Learning by Doing*, 2015; IMF *Finance & Development*, March 2015) identified a Jevons Paradox. ATMs reduced tellers per urban branch from **~21 to ~13** (1988–2004), cutting operating costs sufficiently that banks opened more branches — urban branches increased **43%** over the same period. Concurrent banking deregulation (Riegle-Neal Act, 1994) enabled interstate branching; multi-state banks grew from 10 (1994) to 387 (2005). More branches × fewer tellers = net employment growth. The teller role shifted from cash-handling to relationship banking and cross-selling.

**The reversal:** Mobile banking (widespread from ~2010) broke the branch expansion model by eliminating the need for physical visits entirely. Branch numbers began declining (FDIC: **5.3% decrease** 2012–2017), and teller employment entered sustained contraction. The Burning Glass Institute (2024) found employment down **~30% since 2010**, with job postings falling by two-thirds. Only **4%** of tellers transition to higher-paying roles like loan officers.

**Timescale:** 37 years of growth (1970–2007), then 5 years from mobile banking to visible decline (~2007–2012). Total arc: **~55 years** from ATM introduction to current stabilisation.

**Winners and losers:** Winners included banks, consumers, and tellers who upskilled. Losers are post-2010 displaced tellers — the "universal banker" median salary fell from ~$70K to ~$52K (Burning Glass, 2024). Geographic concentration in rural/smaller states where branches persist (highest location quotients: North Dakota, Oklahoma, South Dakota per BLS OEWS 2023).

**Contested:** Critics argue branch expansion was driven primarily by deregulation, not ATM-enabled cost savings. Rosales (Berkeley, 2018) found the teller-to-labour-force ratio declined every decade after 1970–1980. David Oks (2025) notes "at the exact moment people started talking about how technology had not displaced bank tellers, it stopped being true."

**ISCO-08: 4211** (Bank tellers and related clerks)

### 2. Spreadsheets → Accountants/bookkeepers (1979–2000s) — Type 1: Market expansion

**Prediction:** "Accountants would become obsolete. Secretaries would be eliminated." VisiCalc compressed 20-hour financial models into 15 minutes.

**What happened:** A sharp bifurcation. **400,000 fewer** accounting clerks than in 1980, but **600,000 more** professional accountants — from ~339,000 combined (1980, BLS via Tim Harford, *FT*) to **~1.4 million** accountants/auditors by 2022 (BLS OEWS, SOC 13-2011). Bookkeeper employment (SOC 43-3031) grew **6.4%** even from 1997–2007 (BLS *Beyond the Numbers*, 2018) before entering gradual decline (BLS projects **-6%** from 2024–2034).

**Mechanism:** Classic computation→advisory shift with market expansion. Spreadsheets automated routine calculation, but made accounting **10× more productive** — and that productivity was absorbed by doing more accounting, not employing fewer accountants. "Make an accountant 10× more productive, and you don't need 1/10th as many accountants. You do 10× as much accounting." Previously unaffordable small-business accounting became feasible. Simultaneously, regulatory expansion (Sarbanes-Oxley 2002, Dodd-Frank 2010) created new demand independent of technology effects. The profession shifted from computation to advisory, tax planning, forensic accounting, and compliance.

**Timescale:** VisiCalc (1979) to clear structural shift: ~20 years (1980–2000). Ongoing — 1.5 billion people now use spreadsheets globally.

**Winners:** Professional accountants/auditors (demand exploded), financial analysts, small businesses gaining access. **Losers:** Manual bookkeepers, typing pools, calculation-focused clerical workers.

**ISCO-08: 2411** (Accountants), **4311** (Accounting and bookkeeping clerks)

### 3. Containerisation → Dockworkers (1956–1980s) — Type 2: Full task automation

**Prediction:** ILA leader Thomas Gleason: "The container is digging our graves." Longshoreman labour accounted for **60–75%** of cargo transport costs (Levinson, *The Box*).

**What happened:** **90%+ workforce reduction**. NYC alone employed **>50,000** longshoremen in the early 1950s; by the late 1980s, the ILA workforce had fallen 90%. In the UK, registered dock workers fell from a peak of **82,000** (Hansard) to under **9,400** by 1989 — an **88.5% reduction**. Productivity jumped from **1.5 to 37.5 tons** per worker-hour, a **25× increase**.

**Mechanism:** Containers standardised cargo handling, replacing gangs of 20+ manual workers with crane operators and small crews. The entire "task bundle" was automated — there were no remaining human tasks to preserve. Critically, **geographic displacement** compounded job loss: containers required deep-water ports with flat storage and road/rail links, rendering traditional ports (London Docks, Liverpool, Brooklyn) physically obsolete. New ports — Felixstowe, Elizabeth/Newark, Rotterdam — captured traffic.

Unions negotiated managed decline rather than resistance. The ILWU's **Mechanisation and Modernisation Agreement (1960)** guaranteed no layoffs for existing "A men," achieved workforce reduction through attrition, and funded early retirement (~$29 million). The ILA secured a **Guaranteed Annual Income** and **container royalty funds**, plus a hiring freeze (no new hires 1964–1977). The UK's National Dock Labour Scheme (1947) protected registered workers until Thatcher abolished it in 1989 with £35,000 severance packages.

**Timescale:** McLean's first container ship (1956) to near-complete workforce transformation: **~25 years**.

**Geographic scarring:** El-Sahli & Upward ("Off the Waterfront," *British Journal of Industrial Relations*, 2017) found port districts in England and Wales had higher employment than non-port districts in 1971, but **significantly lower employment 20 years later — a deficit persisting 40 years on** through 2011. London Docklands were eventually redeveloped (Canary Wharf), but Liverpool lost dominance permanently. Rotterdam rose partly because UK port militancy drove traffic to the Netherlands.

**Winners:** Shipping companies, consumers (cargo handling costs fell >90%), remaining workers (ILWU average $171,000/year; ILA NY/NJ >$150,000/year), new port communities. **Losers:** Manual dockworkers, casual "B men" (bore disproportionate losses), traditional port communities.

**ISCO-08: 9333** (Freight handlers) | **⚠️ Data note:** The "90% reduction" is widely cited but sourcing varies; El-Sahli & Upward is the most rigorous quantitative source.

### 4. CAD → Technical drafters (1985–2005) — Type 3: Role absorption

**Prediction:** "Projections indicate CAD could displace over half of drafters by 1990" (IEEE, Majchrzak & Salzman). CAD productivity gains of 5–20× over manual methods.

**What happened:** Drafter employment initially **increased nearly threefold** in automobile manufacturing from 1977–1986 (IEEE study), then peaked at approximately **~326,000** around 1990 before declining **~40%** to **192,100** in 2024 (BLS OOH, SOC 17-3010). BLS projects **0% change** to 2034 — the occupation has stabilised at roughly 60% of peak.

**Mechanism:** This is the paradigmatic "role absorption" case. The BLS states explicitly: "These technologies increase drafter productivity and allow **engineers and architects to perform many tasks that used to be done by drafters**." In Phase 1 (1982–1995), CAD augmented existing drafters' productivity and enabled more design iterations. In Phase 2 (1995–2010), increasingly user-friendly interfaces (AutoCAD 2000, Revit) meant engineers drafted their own work. In Phase 3 (2010–present), Building Information Modelling (BIM) created new specialist roles (BIM Coordinator, VDC Specialist) while further eroding pure drafting.

There was limited market expansion — unlike accounting, cheaper drafting did not create proportionally more demand for design. The dominant effect was "same output, faster." Digital files also enabled offshoring of commodity 2D CAD work.

**Timescale:** AutoCAD (1982) to peak employment: ~8 years. Peak to current stabilisation: ~30 years. **Total: ~40 years**.

**Winners:** Engineers and architects (gained direct design capability), BIM specialists. **Losers:** Mid-career manual drafters, drafting education programmes. Surviving roles are higher-skilled — median wage $65,380, above the $49,500 all-occupation median.

**ISCO-08: 3118** (Draughtspersons) | **⚠️ Data note:** Pre-1996 BLS occupational data for drafters requires Census microdata with crosswalk adjustments; precise historical time series is difficult to reconstruct.

### 5. Automatic switching → Telephone operators (1920s–1980s) — Type 2: Full task automation

**Prediction:** In 1964, economist Elizabeth Faulkner Baker predicted "the decline in the relative importance of telephone operators may be nearing an end," arguing "no machines will be devised" for the array of operator tasks. One year later, Bell installed its first fully electronic switching system.

**What happened:** AT&T operators peaked at **~350,000** in the late 1940s (98% women). Including ~1 million PBX operators at offices and hospitals, roughly **1 in 13 working women** was a telephone operator circa 1950. Employment fell to **<250,000** by 1960, **~40,000** in telecom by 1984, and approximately **23,800** switchboard operators total by 2023 (BLS OES). The UK peaked later — **57,000 operators in 1967** — because demand growth partly offset automation.

**Mechanism:** The paradox of this case is the **86-year diffusion time** from the Strowger automatic switch (1892) to AT&T's fully automated network (1978). Three factors slowed adoption: (1) early electromechanical switching had **increasing unit costs with network scale** — small independent carriers automated first, while big-city Bell companies found it costlier; (2) AT&T's monopoly eliminated competitive pressure from independents who were early adopters; and (3) operators provided valued personal service (wake-up calls, emergencies, directory assistance) that AT&T considered a competitive advantage (Feigenbaum & Gross, *Management Science*, 2024).

Feigenbaum & Gross (*QJE*, 2024) found that after dial cutover, employment of 16–25-year-old women as operators fell **50–80%** immediately. **Incumbent operators** were worse off a decade later — less likely to be working, and if working, in lower-paying occupations. But **future cohorts** were not harmed overall, as declining operator jobs were offset by growing clerical and service occupations.

**Timescale:** Automatic switch (1892) → 32% Bell phones on dial (1930) → entire AT&T network automatic (1978) → Bryant Pond, Maine, last hand-crank system (1983). **~86–91 years total**.

**Winners:** AT&T, consumers, future cohorts of young women absorbed into expanding clerical economy. **Losers:** Incumbent operators, small-town operators with few local alternatives. **Racial dimension:** AT&T did not hire Black operators until after 1940, so automation displaced an overwhelmingly white female workforce.

**ISCO-08: 4223** (Telephone switchboard operators)

### 6. Automatic elevators → Elevator operators (1900s–1960s) — Type 2: Full task automation (friction case)

**Prediction:** No specific predictions survive, but the contemporary assumption was that operators were indispensable — the public viewed elevator operation as a skilled, safety-critical task.

**What happened:** Employment peaked at **114,473** in the 1950 US Census (Atkinson & Wu, ITIF 2017, IPUMS data), then fell to **essentially zero by 1990**. This is the **only one of 270 detailed occupations from the 1950 Census that has been completely eliminated by automation** (James Bessen, Harvard).

**Mechanism:** The central lesson is the 50-year gap between technology availability (~1900) and widespread adoption (~1950s–60s). Automatic elevators existed from approximately 1900, but four forms of friction delayed adoption: (1) **public fear** — given the choice between stairs and an unmanned elevator, the elevator stayed empty; (2) **union resistance** — building service Local 32-B (now 32BJ SEIU) was powerful in NYC; (3) **building codes** requiring attendants; and (4) genuine safety concerns with early systems.

The **1945 NYC elevator operators' strike** was the pivotal catalyst. ~15,000 operators struck, stranding 1.5 million office workers and causing **$100 million in economic losses** ($8 million/day in lost federal tax revenue). Building owners accelerated investment in automation. Otis's Autotronic system (1948–1950) addressed safety with automatic door sensors, emergency phones, and the soothing "Elevoice" recorded announcements. A trust-building campaign showed children and grandmothers riding safely. Adoption went from **12.6% of new installations (1950) to >90% by 1959** — once social barriers broke, the S-curve was steep.

**Timescale:** Technology available (~1900) → 1945 strike catalyst → >90% new installations automated (1959) → occupation essentially zero (~1990). **~90 years total, with the critical adoption window compressed to ~10 years** (1950–1959).

**ISCO-08:** No current code; closest historical equivalent within Major Group 9 (Elementary Occupations). | **⚠️ Data note:** No rigorous academic study tracks elevator operator transition outcomes comparable to Feigenbaum & Gross on telephone operators. Significant data gap.

### 7. Desktop publishing → Typesetters/compositors (1985–2000) — Type 2: Full task automation

**Prediction:** Print unions initially believed they could negotiate technology adoption terms, not prevent it. In practice, the speed of elimination surprised everyone.

**What happened:** The International Typographical Union peaked at **~100,000 members** in the 1960s. By 1986, it had **40,000 working printers + 35,000 retirees** — membership halved between 1984–1987. The ITU dissolved on December 31, 1986. Approximately **4,000 typesetting companies** across North America in 1985 declined to "only a few" by 1995 (Frank Romano, WhatTheyThink). The BLS successor category "Desktop Publishers" (SOC 43-9031) employed only **~5,000** by 2024, projected to decline a further 12%.

The **Wapping dispute (1986)** is the defining moment for the UK case. Rupert Murdoch sacked **~5,500–6,000** production staff and moved operations overnight to a new plant in Wapping that produced identical newspapers with just **~500 employees**. News International pre-tax profits soared from **£16 million (1986) to £124 million (1988)** at a 42% margin. Within years, nearly all national newspapers abandoned Fleet Street.

**Mechanism:** Desktop publishing (PageMaker 1985, PostScript, laser printers) enabled journalists, designers, and office workers to perform typesetting directly. This eliminated the "double keystroke" process where authors wrote and compositors re-keyed. Unlike the Linotype revolution of the 1880s — which initially displaced 60–80% of typesetters but saw employment recover as demand for printed material exploded — DTP coincided with electronic media competition, so **no compensating market expansion occurred**. The entire task bundle was automated, and the output market was simultaneously shrinking.

**Timescale:** PageMaker (1985) to near-extinction: **~10–15 years**. Wapping showed the transition could be **overnight** when combined with political will and Thatcher-era anti-union legislation.

**Workers:** Most displaced Fleet Street workers did **not** transition to DTP — the roles required different skills and were filled by graphic designers and younger workers. Quality also suffered: designers who previously "attacked proofs with a plethora of changes suddenly lowered their standards" when they became their own typesetters (Romano).

**ISCO-08: 7321** (Pre-press technicians)

### 8. Industrial robots → Manufacturing assembly (1960s–present) — Type 1/Type 2 hybrid (varies by country)

**Prediction:** Automation would hollow out manufacturing employment.

**What happened:** US manufacturing employment fell from a peak of **19.6 million (1979)** to **12.6 million (February 2026)** — a **36% decline** (FRED, CES3000000001). But the timing is critical: employment held remarkably stable at 17–18 million from 1970–2000, then **collapsed by 6 million** in just one decade (2000–2010). Meanwhile, Japan and Germany adopted **far more robots** but maintained stronger manufacturing employment.

**Robot density (IFR World Robotics 2024):** South Korea **1,012** robots per 10,000 manufacturing workers; Germany **429**; Japan **419**; United States only **295**; global average **162**. Germany has ~4× the US robot density historically yet maintains manufacturing at **~25% of employment** versus <9% in the US.

**Mechanism — the crucial divergence:**

Acemoglu & Restrepo (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2020) found one more robot per 1,000 US workers reduces the employment-to-population ratio by **0.18–0.34 percentage points** and wages by **0.25–0.5%**. But they acknowledged that **Chinese trade displaced three times as many US jobs as robots** during 1990–2007. The decomposition of automation versus offshoring remains deeply contested — EPI attributes most 2000–2010 losses to trade; Ball State attributes 88% to productivity gains; Upjohn Institute critiques both.

**Germany's complementarity model:** Dauth et al. (2017, CEPR) found "robots have had **no aggregate effect on German employment**." IG Metall and works councils negotiated flexible wage arrangements — workers accepted wage restraint in exchange for job security. Co-determination rights gave works councils voice over personnel planning and training. Workforce reduction occurred through natural attrition rather than layoffs. Germany also manufactures robots (Kuka), so robot-making itself creates employment.

**Japan's positive employment effect:** Adachi, Kawaguchi & Saito (*Journal of Labor Economics*, 2024) found an increase of one robot unit per 1,000 workers **increases** Japanese employment by **2.2%** — the opposite of the US finding. The productivity/scale effect dominated: robots lowered costs → expanded exports → increased labour demand. Lifetime employment (shūshin koyō) removed displacement fear. A declining working-age population (down 13% from 1995 peak) means robots fill shortages rather than displace workers.

**Timescale:** First industrial robot (Unimate at GM, 1961) to current: **65 years and ongoing**. Impact detectable from 1990 but the sharp US employment collapse (2000–2010) was primarily trade-driven.

**Geographic scarring:** US Rust Belt (Michigan, Ohio, Indiana, Pennsylvania) bore disproportionate losses, linked to declining health outcomes, reduced marriage rates, and increased deaths of despair (NBER WP 24468). UK Midlands experienced similar effects.

**ISCO-08: 8210** (Mechanical machinery assemblers) | **⚠️ Data heavily contested:** The automation-vs-offshoring decomposition is the most debated question in this field. EPI, Ball State, Upjohn, and Acemoglu & Restrepo reach significantly different conclusions.

### 9. GPS/ride-hailing → Taxi dispatchers/drivers (2000s–2010s) — Type 4: Market restructuring + Type 3: Role absorption

**Prediction:** No significant labour-market predictions preceded this disruption. It emerged from the combination of GPS, smartphones, and platform business models rather than a single technology.

**What happened:** Taxi dispatchers were **quietly automated** — only 4,510 remain in taxi/limo services (BLS OES 2023, SOC 43-5032). The more dramatic story is restructuring: ride-hailing nonemployer establishments surged from **176,000 (2010) to 480,000 (2015)** (Census Bureau Nonemployer Statistics). By December 2015, Uber alone had **~463,000** active US drivers. But new entrant earnings collapsed — 2013 incumbents earned $41,840 while 2015 new entrants earned only **$11,450** (Census), with 73% holding another job. Berger, Chen & Frey (*European Economic Review*, 2018) found Uber entry reduced incumbent taxi driver hourly earnings by **10–17%**.

**London's Knowledge under threat:** Licensed London black cabs fell **34.5%** from 22,810 (2013–14) to 14,470 (2023–24) (TfL data). New Knowledge applications crashed **~95%**: from 3,484 (2011–12) to just 221 (2021). Meanwhile, London's Uber drivers nearly doubled from 25,000 (2016) to 45,000 (2018). UCL neuroscience research (Maguire et al., 2000; 2011) confirmed The Knowledge causes measurable posterior hippocampal growth — a form of human cognitive capital being made obsolete by GPS.

**NYC medallion crash:** Peak value **~$1.3 million** (individual medallion, 2013) → crash to **~$80,000–200,000** by 2018 (up to 90% loss). Total medallion loan book: ~$4.9 billion. Credit union failures cost NCUA **~$750 million**. At least **nine NYC taxi driver suicides** attributed to financial distress. 90% of NYC taxi drivers are immigrants — the wealth destruction fell disproportionately on immigrant communities.

**Timescale:** Uber launch (2010) to major medallion crash: **~3–5 years**. The fastest disruption in this sample.

**ISCO-08: 8322** (Car, taxi and van drivers), **4323** (Transport clerks)

### 10. E-commerce → Retail salespeople (2000s–present) — Type 1 (transitioning to Type 4)

**Prediction:** The "retail apocalypse" narrative (gained currency ~2017) predicted mass store closures and job elimination.

**What happened:** US retail trade employment actually **grew ~8.1%** from 2010–2017, reaching approximately **15,800–15,900 thousand** — despite e-commerce growing from 4.2% to ~11% of retail sales over the same period. The turning point was **~2017**: traditional retail lost **~200,000 jobs** between 2017–2019, then COVID accelerated losses with **~800,000 jobs** lost in 2020. As of February 2026, retail trade employment stands at **15,427 thousand** (FRED), below pre-pandemic levels. E-commerce reached **16.4%** of total US retail ($1.234 trillion) in 2025 (Census Bureau).

**The "apocalypse" was concentrated, not universal.** IHL Group (2019) found just 16 companies were responsible for 54% of all 2017 store closures. For every retailer closing stores, more than **five retail chains were opening stores**. Department stores (Sears: 3,500 stores → bankruptcy 2018 with 687) and mall-dependent specialty apparel bore the brunt. Grocery employment reached a record **2.6 million** in 2023.

**The structural substitution:** Warehousing/storage employment grew **86.1%** and couriers/messengers grew **54.4%** from 2010–2019 (BLS *Beyond the Numbers*), versus retail trade's 8.1%. The labour market is shifting from store-based to logistics-based employment, but whether this is net-positive remains debated. EPI (Jones & Zipperer, 2018) found Amazon fulfilment centres produced ~30% increases in local warehousing employment but **no net increase in county-wide overall employment**.

**UK high street:** Far worse than the US. GMB union analysis of ONS data shows **420,242 bricks-and-mortar retail jobs lost** since 2010 — a **28.4% decline**. UK online retail penetration is significantly higher (~27% vs US ~16%). Average of 47 store closures daily (PwC, 2023). High street foot traffic down **40% since 2012** (Springboard).

**Timescale:** Amazon (1994) to e-commerce <1% of retail (2000) to 16.4% (2025): **~25 years of gradual substitution**, with COVID providing a 3-year acceleration shock.

**ISCO-08: 5223** (Shop sales assistants)

## The disruption pattern taxonomy

### Type 1 — Partial task automation → job transformation + market expansion

**Defining cases:** ATMs→tellers (Phase 1), spreadsheets→accountants, industrial robots in Japan/Germany

**Diagnostic signature:** Technology automates **some but not all** core tasks. Cost reduction creates sufficient demand elasticity to expand the addressable market. Remaining human tasks (relationship banking, advisory judgment, flexible problem-solving) are high-value and resistant to automation. The occupation transforms rather than disappears — workers shift from routine to non-routine tasks.

| Diagnostic variable | Type 1 indicator |
|---|---|
| Task automation completeness | Partial — some tasks automated, others preserved |
| Market demand elasticity | High — cost reduction expands addressable market |
| Remaining human task value | High — judgment, relationships, advisory |
| Physical/relational component | Strong relational or judgment component |
| Technology maturity vs institutional speed | Technology matures gradually; institutions adapt |
| Union/regulatory friction | Neutral to positive — manages transition pace |

**Critical caveat:** Type 1 is often **temporary**. ATMs→tellers became Type 2 when mobile banking automated the remaining tasks. Spreadsheets may follow the same trajectory as AI automates advisory accounting. **The market expansion effect has a half-life** — it persists only as long as some tasks remain uniquely human and the output market remains expandable.

---

### Type 2 — Full task automation → job elimination

**Defining cases:** Containerisation→dockworkers, telephone switchboard operators, elevator operators, DTP→typesetters

**Diagnostic signature:** Technology automates **all or nearly all** core tasks in the occupational bundle. No remaining human tasks justify the standalone role. Market expansion (even if it occurs) does not compensate because labour per unit of output collapses. Employment reductions of **80–100%** over 10–90 years.

| Diagnostic variable | Type 2 indicator |
|---|---|
| Task automation completeness | Total — entire task bundle automated |
| Market demand elasticity | Irrelevant — even if output grows, labour-per-unit collapses |
| Remaining human task value | None or negligible |
| Physical/relational component | None — task is fully mechanisable or digitisable |
| Technology maturity vs institutional speed | Variable — can be very slow (elevator: 90 years) or fast (DTP: 15 years) |
| Union/regulatory friction | Can delay but not prevent elimination; negotiated decline better than resistance |

**Sub-patterns within Type 2:**

The four Type 2 cases reveal important variation in how elimination proceeds:

**Managed decline** (containerisation): Union-negotiated attrition, generous buyouts, hiring freezes. Remaining workers extremely well-compensated ($150K–$171K/year). Geographic scarring persists 40+ years.

**Contested elimination** (DTP/typesetters): Union resistance met with deliberate union-breaking (Wapping). Overnight transition when political conditions aligned. Workers left industry entirely.

**Glacial elimination** (telephone operators, elevator operators): Social trust, institutional inertia, and scaling costs delayed adoption for 50–90 years. Once barriers broke, the S-curve was steep. Future cohorts absorbed into adjacent occupations.

---

### Type 3 — Role absorption: function survives, standalone role disappears

**Defining cases:** CAD→drafters, GPS→taxi dispatchers

**Diagnostic signature:** The **function** the occupation performed continues to be needed, but it is absorbed into an adjacent, higher-skilled role. Engineers draft their own work. Algorithms dispatch their own vehicles. The standalone occupation shrinks 40–90% but may not reach zero, as residual specialist niches persist.

| Diagnostic variable | Type 3 indicator |
|---|---|
| Task automation completeness | Partial — but the *non-automated* tasks belong to an adjacent profession |
| Market demand elasticity | Low to moderate — no major expansion |
| Remaining human task value | High, but assigned to a different (higher-skill) occupation |
| Physical/relational component | Weak — the function is informational/technical |
| Technology maturity vs institutional speed | Technology interface becomes user-friendly enough for non-specialists |
| Union/regulatory friction | Low friction — occupational boundary shift rather than layoffs |

**Distinguishing Type 3 from Type 1:** In Type 1, the same workers transform their roles. In Type 3, **different workers** (engineers, designers) absorb the function. The displaced occupation's workers rarely move "up" into the absorbing role — most exit or take lower-status positions.

---

### Type 4 — Market restructuring: job persists, employment relationship changes

**Defining cases:** GPS/ride-hailing→taxi drivers, e-commerce→retail (emerging)

**Diagnostic signature:** The core task (driving, selling) continues to require human labour, but the **institutional structure** of employment transforms — typically from regulated employment to platform/gig work. The occupation may even grow in headcount while deteriorating in pay, benefits, stability, and worker power.

| Diagnostic variable | Type 4 indicator |
|---|---|
| Task automation completeness | Low — core task still requires human performance |
| Market demand elasticity | High — platform reduces friction, expands market |
| Remaining human task value | Moderate — task is necessary but treated as commodity |
| Physical/relational component | Physical presence required (driving, delivering) |
| Technology maturity vs institutional speed | Technology matures faster than regulatory frameworks adapt |
| Union/regulatory friction | Critical determinant — regulated markets resist restructuring; deregulated markets transform rapidly |

**The Type 4 trap:** Total employment may increase while per-worker welfare decreases. Uber increased total trip supply ~25% in London, but incumbent driver earnings fell 10–17%. NYC taxi medallion holders lost up to **90% of their asset value** — catastrophic wealth destruction concentrated among immigrants.

**European regulatory divergence matters enormously:** Germany's requirement that Uber comply with taxi regulations has partially protected incumbent drivers. France liberalised but retained some protections. The UK sits between — Uber operates freely but the Supreme Court ruled drivers are "workers" (2021).

---

### Type 5 — Demand creation: technology creates entirely new occupations

**Emergent evidence across cases (not a standalone case study):**

Every disruption generated some new occupations, though usually fewer jobs than were destroyed:

- ATMs→ATM technicians, digital banking specialists
- Spreadsheets→financial analysts, data scientists
- Containers→crane operators, logistics coordinators, supply chain managers
- CAD→BIM coordinators, VDC specialists, parametric design engineers
- Automatic elevators→elevator maintenance/repair technicians (**24,200 jobs** at median **$106,580/year**, BLS 2024)
- DTP→desktop publishers (only ~5,000 by 2024 — far fewer than displaced typesetters)
- Ride-hailing→platform operations, surge pricing engineers, safety/trust teams
- E-commerce→warehouse operatives, delivery drivers, e-commerce managers, SEO specialists

**Key pattern:** Demand-created occupations tend to be either (a) significantly higher-skill and fewer in number (BIM specialists, data scientists) or (b) lower-quality and more numerous (warehouse operatives, gig drivers). This produces **hollowing** — the middle disappears.

## The diagnostic framework: predicting disruption patterns

Six structural variables determine which pattern an occupation follows. This framework is designed as a reusable input for Layer 5 AI synthesis.

| Variable | Type 1 (Transform + Expand) | Type 2 (Eliminate) | Type 3 (Absorb) | Type 4 (Restructure) | Type 5 (Create) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Task automation scope** | Partial | Complete | Partial, but remaining tasks belong elsewhere | Low (core task persists) | N/A — new tasks emerge |
| **Market price elasticity** | High (cost↓ → demand↑) | Irrelevant (labour/unit collapses) | Low-moderate | High (friction↓ → demand↑) | New market created |
| **Remaining human task value** | High (judgment, relational) | None | High, but in adjacent role | Moderate (commodity) | High (novel skills) |
| **Physical/relational barrier** | Strong (face-to-face, trust) | None | Weak (informational) | Physical presence required | Variable |
| **Tech maturity vs. institutional speed** | Gradual; institutions keep pace | Variable (10–90 years) | Interface democratises function | Tech outruns regulation | Tech creates new needs |
| **Union/regulatory effect** | Neutral-positive (paces transition) | Delays but cannot prevent | Low impact | Critical determinant of outcome | Minimal role |

**How to apply this to AI-exposed occupations:** For any occupation in the ISCO-based AI exposure map, assess each variable to predict the most likely disruption pattern. The single most important diagnostic question is: **Does the technology automate some tasks (leaving high-value human tasks) or all core tasks?** If partial automation meets high market elasticity, expect Type 1. If partial automation but remaining tasks naturally belong to an adjacent profession, expect Type 3. If all tasks are automated, expect Type 2 regardless of other variables — though timescale will be governed by institutional friction.

## What timescale actually looks like

Perhaps the most policy-relevant finding is how consistently disruption timescales exceed technological capability:

| Case | Technology ready | Employment peak | Near-complete impact | Total span |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elevator operators | ~1900 | 1950 | ~1990 | **~90 years** |
| Telephone operators | 1892 | ~1950 | ~1983 | **~91 years** |
| ATMs→tellers | ~1970 | 2007 | Ongoing | **~55+ years** |
| Containerisation | 1956 | Pre-1956 | ~1980 | **~25 years** |
| Spreadsheets→bookkeepers | 1979 | ~1980 | Ongoing | **~45+ years** |
| CAD→drafters | 1982 | ~1990 | ~2020 (stabilised) | **~40 years** |
| DTP→typesetters | 1985 | ~1960s | ~2000 | **~15 years** |
| Industrial robots (US) | 1961 | 1979 | Ongoing | **~65+ years** |
| GPS/ride-hailing | ~2010 | Pre-2010 | Ongoing | **~15 years** |
| E-commerce→retail | ~2000 | ~2017 | Ongoing | **~25+ years** |

The fastest completed disruptions (DTP, ride-hailing) took **10–15 years**. The slowest took **90 years**. The median is roughly **30–40 years**. Union/regulatory friction, public trust deficits, and complementary infrastructure requirements consistently slow adoption — but they rarely prevent it permanently. **Exogenous shocks** (the 1945 elevator strike, Wapping, COVID) compress timescales dramatically when they align with mature technology.

## Implications for the AI disruption taxonomy

Three critical lessons emerge from these ten cases that should directly inform AI labour-market modelling:

**First, Type 1 is the best-case scenario but may be temporary.** ATMs gave bank tellers 37 years of employment growth before mobile banking triggered decline. If AI initially automates partial tasks in a profession, the market expansion effect may dominate for a decade or more — but the window closes when subsequent AI capabilities automate the remaining human tasks. Analysts should model Type 1 occupations with an explicit **half-life parameter**.

**Second, the Germany/Japan robot story is the strongest evidence that institutions shape outcomes.** The same technology (industrial robots) produced job losses in the US, neutrality in Germany, and employment **gains** in Japan. Works councils, lifetime employment, wage flexibility agreements, and co-determination rights are not peripheral — they are **primary determinants** of whether automation follows a Type 1 or Type 2 pattern. European AI policy should focus on institutional design at least as much as technology regulation.

**Third, geographic scarring is the most persistent damage.** El-Sahli & Upward's finding of 40-year employment deficits in UK port districts — and the parallel Rust Belt/Midlands evidence for manufacturing — suggests that **place-based** impacts outlast occupation-level impacts by decades. Workers eventually find new occupations; communities do not always recover. AI exposure mapping should weight geographic concentration of vulnerable occupations as a first-order risk factor, particularly for European regions with occupational monocultures.
