# D.1 Corrected — The Substitution Question: AI displacement is delayed, demographic withdrawal is not

**Correction context:** The original D.1 section (in "Europe is running out of workers, not work") overstated near-term AI displacement in Zone A by treating it as a current phenomenon. Three primary sources — Acemoglu (NBER w32487, May 2024), the ECB SAFE survey (Lebastard & Sondermann, March 2026), and the ifo Business Survey (2025) — together establish that AI displacement is real but **temporally delayed**, while demographic replacement demand is happening now and accelerating. Cedefop Skills Forecast 2025 provides modelled replacement demand numbers. Eurostat LFS (lfsa_egais, 2024) provides the actual current workforce age structure by ISCO occupation group, confirming the scale of near-retirement cohorts.

---

Cedefop's Skills Forecast 2025 provides the actual modelled replacement demand by ISCO occupation group, computed from Eurostat LFS, National Accounts, and EUROPOP projections. These are not retirements only — they include all permanent workforce exits (retirement, death, emigration, career exit).

## The displacement timeline is slower than the demographic timeline

Three papers establish that AI is not yet displacing workers in Europe — it is currently a net hiring driver:

### ECB SAFE Survey (March 2026, ~5,300 euro area firms)

- Two-thirds of firms report employees using AI; only **25% invest** in AI (most use free tools)
- Companies with significant AI use are **~4% more likely to hire** additional staff
- Firms investing in AI are **~2% more likely to hire** than non-investors
- Firms using AI for R&D/innovation: positive hiring effect
- Firms using AI to reduce personnel costs: **negative** hiring and positive layoff effect — but only **15% of AI-using firms** cite cost reduction as their purpose
- Forward-looking (1-year): firms planning AI investment expect **higher** employment growth
- **Net effect today: AI is a hiring accelerant, not a displacement force**

### ifo Business Survey (2025, German firms)

- **25% of German companies** expect AI to lead to job cuts — but over a **5-year horizon**, not immediately
- This bridges the ECB's near-term positive signal with the medium-term displacement concern
- The displacement timeline is 2027–2030, not 2024–2026

### Acemoglu (NBER w32487, May 2024)

- Applies Hulten's theorem to task-level AI exposure data
- Upper bound TFP gain: **0.66% over 10 years** (using Eloundou et al. task exposure estimates)
- More realistic estimate: **≤0.53%** — because current evidence comes from "easy-to-learn" tasks; hard-to-learn tasks with context-dependent judgment show much less impact
- For context: Goldman Sachs (Briggs & Kodnani, 2023) predicts 7% GDP impact / 1.5pp annual productivity. McKinsey projects $17–25T. **Acemoglu argues these are implausible.**
- AI impact is more equally distributed across demographics than previous automation (robots hit manufacturing men hardest; AI exposure is broadly spread)
- AI is predicted to widen the **capital-vs-labour income gap**, not reduce labour inequality

### Albanesi et al. (NBER w31357, 2023) — European empirical evidence

- Examines 16 European countries, 3-digit ISCO level, 2011–2019
- Occupations with higher AI/software exposure **increased** employment share
- Effect strongest for younger, highly skilled workers (consistent with Skill-Biased Technological Change)
- **Very few countries** show employment decline in AI-exposed occupations
- Country variation linked to technology diffusion pace, education, and **employment protection legislation** (strong in DACH)

## The Jevons paradox for Zone A — why the original analysis was too pessimistic

The original D.1 assumed Zone A faces "surplus displacement" — AI replaces faster than workers retire, creating 5–10M displaced workers. This ignores the ATM/teller pattern documented in Layer 3's own micro-disruption analysis.

**The ATM pattern (Layer 3, Type 1 disruption):** When ATMs automated cash-handling tasks, bank teller employment *grew* — because cheaper branch operations expanded the addressable market, and tellers shifted to relationship banking. Employment peaked at 608,000 in 2007, up from 268,000 in 1970 (BLS OEWS, SOC 43-3071).

**Applied to Zone A:** If AI automates 60–80% of clerical tasks but makes clerical services dramatically cheaper, demand for clerical output may expand. A small business that couldn't afford a bookkeeper can now afford AI-assisted bookkeeping. The occupation transforms (data entry clerk → data quality manager; bookkeeper → financial analyst with AI tools) rather than vanishes.

**The counter-argument:** The ATM pattern worked because tellers had residual high-value tasks (relationship banking). If AI automates *all* core tasks in a clerical bundle — and generative AI is closer to this than ATMs were — the expansion effect may be weaker. Containerisation (Type 2, 90%+ workforce reduction) is the alternative pattern.

**The empirical evidence so far favours transformation over elimination.** Georgieff & Milanez (OECD, 2021) found that historically high-exposure occupations experienced employment growth. Albanesi et al. found the same in European data. The ECB SAFE survey shows AI-using firms hiring. But these are early signals — the technology is still in its adoption phase, not its restructuring phase.

**Corrected estimate for Zone A displacement:** Instead of "5–10M surplus displaced," the evidence supports:
- **2024–2027:** Net zero to positive employment effect (ECB finding). AI absorbs natural attrition (~1.8M Zone A exits over 3 years). Some role transformation.
- **2027–2030:** Net displacement begins as AI matures. ifo data suggests 25% of German firms plan cuts. At EU scale: **1–3M net Zone A displacement** over this period (not 5–10M), partially absorbed by concurrent retirement of ~2.4M Zone A workers.
- **2030–2035:** Displacement accelerates if AI capability continues its current trajectory. But by this point, the demographic constraint dominates — the question shifts from "where do displaced workers go?" to "where do we find workers at all?"

## Bottom line (corrected)

The original D.1 headline — "AI over-substitutes where retirement is manageable, and cannot substitute where retirement is catastrophic" — **remains directionally correct** but was too aggressive on timing and magnitude for Zone A.

The corrected version:

1. **Zone C gap is confirmed and precisely quantified.** Cedefop projects **23.0M** workers leaving Zone C occupations by 2030. Eurostat confirms **21.9M** Zone C workers are currently aged 55+ (23.6% of the zone). Agriculture is the most exposed sector: **33.4%** aged 55+. AI can substitute at most 5–15% of these tasks. The gap is real, large, and unfillable by technology.

2. **Zone A displacement is delayed, not cancelled.** AI is currently a net hiring driver in Europe (ECB, 2026). Displacement begins ~2027–2030 (ifo), but at lower magnitude than originally estimated (1–3M net, not 5–10M), constrained by Acemoglu's TFP ceiling (≤0.53% over 10 years) and the Jevons paradox. Critically, **4.6M Zone A workers are aged 55+** (23.3%) — natural attrition alone absorbs most plausible displacement scenarios through 2030.

3. **The binding constraint is Zone C, not Zone A.** Zone C replacement demand (23.0M by 2030, 39.2M by 2035) is **5.5× larger** than Zone A replacement demand (4.2M by 2030). Europe's policy priority should be filling Zone C gaps (immigration, robotics, work redesign), not managing Zone A displacement that may partially self-correct through market expansion and natural attrition.

4. **Germany is the acute case.** 30.1% of German clerks (Zone A) and 29.3% of German Zone C workers are aged 55+ — the highest rates in the EU. Germany needs 400,000+ net migrants annually just to maintain workforce levels, yet its 163 Engpassberufe are overwhelmingly in Zone C occupations where a third of workers will retire within a decade. The arithmetic is unforgiving: Germany will lose ~10.5M workers to replacement demand by 2030 (Cedefop), of which 4.6M are in Zone C.

5. **The temporal mismatch is the real risk.** Retirement is happening now (~6.7M workers leave EU-27 occupations per year, every year, with certainty). AI displacement is coming but on a 3–5 year delay. The window where retirement outpaces AI displacement is closing — but it hasn't closed yet. This creates a brief opportunity (2025–2028) to manage the transition before both forces compound.
