# Europe is running out of workers, not work

**AI and demographics are colliding in European labour markets, but not in the way most forecasts assume.** The continent's core challenge is not mass technological unemployment — it is a structural mismatch between where AI eliminates jobs and where retiring workers leave unfillable gaps. By 2030, an estimated **10–14 million Zone C workers** (healthcare, trades, construction, care) will retire from the EU-27 workforce in occupations where AI can substitute at most 5–15% of tasks, while AI simultaneously over-saturates displacement capacity in the **25–30 million** clerical and administrative roles where retirement pressure is comparatively modest. This asymmetry — AI strongest where need is least, weakest where need is greatest — defines the coming decade. Europe's binding constraint is workforce availability, not job creation. The ~1 million annual decline in working-age population is demographically locked in, and neither AI, immigration, nor retraining can independently resolve it.

---

## D.1 — AI over-substitutes where retirement is manageable, and cannot substitute where retirement is catastrophic

The substitution question asks whether AI can fill the gaps left by Europe's retirement wave. The answer splits sharply by zone. Mapping ISCO-08 occupation groups to AI exposure zones yields an EU-27 workforce of roughly **215 million**, distributed unevenly: Zone C (low AI exposure — trades, healthcare, construction, agriculture, care, emergency services) accounts for roughly **90–100 million** workers, making it by far the largest zone. Zone B (medium AI, transformation — software, marketing, management, HR) holds **40–50 million**. Zone A (high AI exposure — clerical, admin, customer service, bookkeeping) contains **25–30 million**. Zone D (AI-enhanced demand — AI/ML engineering, data science) remains small at **3–5 million** but is growing rapidly.

The age distribution within these zones is decisive. Zone C has both the **highest share of workers aged 55+** (22–28%) and the largest absolute numbers, putting **20–26 million** workers within a decade of retirement. Agriculture leads with roughly **30%** of workers aged 55+, followed by construction and skilled trades at 22–25%. By contrast, Zone A has approximately **20–22%** aged 55+ (~5–6.5 million), and the ICT-heavy Zone D has just 10–14%. The retirement wave hits hardest precisely where AI has the least substitution capacity.

**The substitution matrix**

| Zone | EU-27 employment | Workers 55+ | Retirements by 2030 | Retirements by 2040 | AI displacement potential | Net workforce dynamic |
|------|-----------------|-------------|---------------------|---------------------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| **A** (High AI) | 25–30M | 5–6.5M | 3–4M | 8–10M | High (60–80% of tasks) | **Surplus displacement**: AI replaces faster than retirement creates gaps; ~5–10M mid-career workers at risk |
| **B** (Medium AI) | 40–50M | 8–10M | 4–6M | 12–16M | Medium (20–35% of tasks) | **Partial offset**: AI fills ~1–2M roles via productivity; ~2–4M transformed; genuine shortage in strategic roles |
| **C** (Low AI) | 90–100M | 20–26M | 10–14M | 28–36M | Low (5–15% of tasks) | **Critical gap**: ~8–12M unfilled by 2030; ~24–32M by 2040; AI cannot compensate |
| **D** (AI-enhanced) | 3–5M | 0.3–0.5M | 0.2–0.3M | 0.5–1M | N/A (demand creation) | **Chronic undersupply**: EU has 10M ICT specialists but needs 20M by 2030 |

The ILO's 2023 analysis found that **82% of clerical tasks** (24% high exposure, 58% medium) are exposed to generative AI — making Zone A the only zone where AI can plausibly exceed retirement-driven attrition. This creates a compounding effect: rather than filling gaps, AI accelerates displacement of mid-career Zone A workers who are **years from retirement**. Data entry clerks, payroll clerks, and administrative secretaries face the most acute double pressure. The OECD estimates **27% of total OECD employment** is at highest automation risk, while the IMF puts approximately **60%** of advanced-economy jobs at some AI exposure — though only about half of those face net negative effects.

**Bottom line for D.1:** Europe's retirement wave and AI capability are almost perfectly misaligned. In Zone A, AI creates a displacement surplus — more workers pushed out than retire. In Zone C, retirement creates a gap AI cannot fill: **8–12 million positions by 2030** in healthcare, construction, trades, and care. The 2024 European Commission Ageing Report projects labour's contribution to GDP turning negative in the late 2020s. This is not a forecast — it is arithmetic.

## D.2 — The cognitive-to-physical transition has almost no historical precedent and cannot work at scale

If AI displaces millions of Zone A clerical workers while Zone C faces millions of unfilled positions, the obvious policy response is retraining. The evidence says this will fail at anything approaching the necessary scale.

**Occupational mobility in Europe is remarkably low.** A major IZA study using EU-SILC microdata across 25+ countries found that only **~3% of European workers** change occupation category (2-digit ISCO) in any given year, ranging from 0.5% in Romania to 7.4% in Sweden. Employment protection legislation — strong across DACH — is a key determinant of this low mobility. Critically, occupation changers are more likely to experience **downward** than upward earnings transitions, and involuntary switchers (which AI-displaced workers would be) fare significantly worse than voluntary ones.

The proposed Zone A→C transition faces a direction problem no previous technological disruption has tested. The farm-to-manufacturing transition (1880–1940) moved workers toward **higher** wages and better conditions. NBER research shows **63%** of that agricultural employment decrease came from factories opening in rural areas, not workers migrating to retrain. The manufacturing-to-services transition was similarly generational — young people chose different careers, rather than displaced factory workers retraining as office workers. The knowledge-worker-to-physical-labour transition asks people to move **downward** in pay, status, and working conditions. This pattern is essentially without historical precedent at scale.

**Germany's retraining infrastructure is substantial but wrongly configured.** A full Umschulung (vocational retraining) takes **2 years** full-time, with IAB research showing employment benefits of **+8 percentage points** versus non-participants appearing only after a 3+ year lock-in period. Healthcare retraining shows the strongest effects, especially for women (+20 percentage points). The cost ranges from **$3,500 to $25,000** per participant. But the OECD has explicitly noted Germany's system lacks "the kind of substantial occupational retraining that may be needed in the context of digitalisation and structural change." The system was designed for incremental upskilling within existing career tracks, not radical cross-zone pivots.

**The throughput mathematics are unforgiving.** Even with Germany's 163 Engpassberufe (shortage occupations) and ~439,000 unfilled skilled positions, only **one in four** unemployed skilled workers seeks a job in a shortage occupation. Germany had 2.7 million unemployed in 2024 but could not fill positions. This is not primarily a skills problem — it reflects preferences, geography, working conditions, and compensation. Drywall construction vacancies in Germany average **299 days** unfilled.

The age trap makes this worse. OECD data shows only **24%** of workers aged 55–65 participate in job-related training, versus 48% for ages 35–54. Workers displaced after age 50 face **significantly higher risks of long-term unemployment**. The employment rate gap between workers aged 55–64 (64.6%) and those aged 25–54 (80.1%) exceeds **15 percentage points** across the OECD. Physical capacity adds another barrier: Zone C occupations demand sustained physical work, and less than half of workers without higher education are predicted to have capacity for full-time physical work by age 62–64. An IMF study found that each percentage point increase in the share of workers aged 55–64 reduces output-per-worker growth by **0.25–0.7 percentage points**.

**Bottom line for D.2:** Cross-zone retraining from cognitive to physical occupations will work for a small minority — perhaps younger displaced workers (under 45) entering healthcare or care coordination roles. At scale, the mathematics forbid it. A 3% annual occupational switching rate, 2-year retraining programmes, collapsing retraining effectiveness after age 50, and workers' rational reluctance to accept lower-paid physical work mean this mismatch is **structurally unresolvable** through domestic labour market policy alone.

## D.3 — AI does not solve Europe's immigration arithmetic, and politics may prevent immigration from solving it either

Europe needs roughly **1 million additional workers per year** to maintain workforce levels through 2050. Germany alone requires **400,000+ annual net migrants**. AI simultaneously threatens millions of cognitive/clerical jobs. The critical question is whether AI displacement reduces the total immigration need — and the answer is: barely.

The structural mismatch between AI's impact zone and immigration's contribution zone is nearly complete. In Germany, immigrants account for **67%** of the construction and drywall workforce, **over 50%** of food service, **46%** of bus and tram drivers, and **30%** of nursing home staff. Non-citizens accounted for **89%** of the 1.6 million net increase in socially insured employment between 2018 and 2023. Foreign-born doctors doubled from ~30,000 in 2013 to **63,763** by 2023, comprising ~14% of healthcare professionals. These are overwhelmingly Zone C occupations where AI has minimal substitution capacity.

AI displacement concentrates in Zone A — bookkeeping, administrative support, customer service — occupations where immigrants are **not** disproportionately represented. The IAB projects **1.6 million** German jobs could be reshaped or lost to AI over 15 years, but these are mostly cognitive/clerical roles. AI may reduce total immigration need by an estimated **5–15%** through eliminating some mid-skill positions, but it has virtually no effect on the sectors driving immigration demand. AI may actually **increase** relative demand for Zone C immigrants as mid-skill jobs disappear while physical and care shortages intensify with population ageing.

Germany's 2024 net migration of approximately **400,000–440,000** roughly meets the target but represents a declining trajectory from the 2022 peak. A critical development: **net EU migration to Germany turned negative in 2024**, making third-country migration essential. The Fachkräfteeinwanderungsgesetz (Skilled Immigration Act) issued over 30,000 EU Blue Cards and 17,489 Opportunity Cards by November 2025, but only **53,000 out of 610,000** residence titles granted to third-country nationals in 2024 were employment-related. The pipeline is not yet calibrated to labour market needs.

**The political constraint may prove more binding than the demographic one.** The AfD won **20.8%** in Germany's February 2025 federal election and now polls at **24.8–26%**, potentially the strongest party. In eastern states, AfD reaches 38–40%. Immigration ranks as the **#1 voter concern**: 56% of Germans name it a top-three issue. Austria's FPÖ won **28.8%** in September 2024 — the first time a far-right party topped an Austrian post-war election — on a platform of "Fortress Austria" and "zero asylum." Switzerland's SVP has launched an initiative to **cap population at 10 million**, with a vote scheduled for June 2026; if approved, it could force termination of EU free movement. All three DACH countries are simultaneously expanding skilled immigration channels and restricting humanitarian pathways — a political tightrope that may not hold.

**Bottom line for D.3:** AI does not solve the immigration arithmetic. It marginally reduces total headcount needs (~5–15%) while having zero effect on the Zone C sectors where immigrants are most concentrated and shortages most acute. Meanwhile, political dynamics across DACH are trending toward restriction precisely when demographic need is intensifying. The paradox is stark: Europe needs more immigrants than ever in exactly the sectors AI cannot touch, while political tolerance for immigration is declining. The Draghi Report's projection of a **41-million working-age decline** by 2070 assumes continued positive net migration — without it, the decline accelerates by an additional **46 million**.

## D.4 — Each generation faces a distinct crisis, and the pipeline is fracturing simultaneously at every level

The generational mapping reveals that AI + demographics does not create a single crisis but **four simultaneous, interlocking ones** — each affecting a different generation through a different mechanism.

**Boomers (62–80, ~15–20M still employed in EU-27): the knowledge exodus.** The 1964 birth cohort — Germany's largest at **1.36 million births** — turned 62 in 2026. Bavaria alone expects to lose **1.5 million workers** by 2035, roughly one-third of the state's workforce. Boomers remaining in Zone A (clerical/admin) face compounded risk: AI displacement arrives simultaneously with retirement proximity, leaving minimal time or incentive for adaptation. But the larger risk is organizational. Only **29%** of organizations incorporate retirement forecasts into knowledge transfer practices, and only **23%** of managers are trained in critical skills transfer. Germany's Mittelstand — family-owned SMEs carrying decades of tacit manufacturing and engineering knowledge — is particularly vulnerable. The boomer exit is demographically inevitable and largely AI-irrelevant; the question is whether institutions capture what leaves with them.

**Gen X (46–61, ~55–60M employed): the frozen middle.** This generation occupies the senior management and professional roles of ISCO 1–2, positions with high AI exposure but also high complementarity — meaning AI transforms rather than eliminates their work. The trap is temporal: Gen X workers have **15–20 years** remaining in their careers but declining training participation rates. The OECD finds older workers are significantly less likely to use advanced ICT than younger colleagues in the same occupations. Only **12%** of Gen X plan 6–10 professional development courses, versus 26% of Gen Z. They are too invested in current career paths for radical pivots, yet face the longest remaining exposure to AI-driven transformation. This generation receives the **least policy attention** despite arguably needing the most support for sustained adaptation.

**Millennials (30–45, ~60–65M employed): maximum exposure, maximum adaptability.** The Bruegel Working Paper 06/2024 confirms that jobs held by **younger, highly educated workers** are most exposed to generative AI across Europe — and millennials dominate Zone B (software, marketing, management, HR, product). They claim **62% AI expertise** versus 50% for Gen Z and receive more AI opportunities at work (44% versus 34%). Indeed's analysis identifies mid-career professionals and managers aged 25–54 as **most at risk** for GenAI disruption. Yet millennials also possess the strongest adaptive capacity: established skills, professional networks, financial resources, and sufficient career runway to justify reinvestment. The millennial question is not whether they survive AI but whether their roles transform productively (Type 1/ATM pattern) or destructively (Type 2/containerisation pattern). Evidence currently favours transformation over elimination for most Zone B roles — CEPR research across 12,000+ European firms found AI adoption increases productivity **~4%** on average with no immediate employment losses.

**Gen Z (14–29, ~30–35M in EU workforce): structural exclusion.** The Ravio 2025 Tech Job Market Report documented a **73% year-over-year decline** in entry-level hiring rates (P1/P2 job levels) in European tech — compared to only 7% decline across all levels. Randstad's global data shows junior tech roles declining **35%** since January 2024. Indeed's European tech postings fell over **40%** from pre-pandemic levels in Switzerland, the UK, and France. EU youth unemployment stands at **15.1%** (January 2026), with Spain at 26.6% and even Germany at 6.6%. Gen Z is not being displaced from jobs — they are being **prevented from entering** them. Only **45%** hold traditional full-time roles, and average job tenure is just **1.1 years**. This generation is nominally "AI-native" — 85% are familiar with generative AI tools, 75% are using AI to upskill — but they paradoxically have **less access** to AI at work than millennials. The entry-level pipeline collapse creates a time-delayed crisis: if junior hiring is cut now, the mid-level talent pool in 2030–2035 will be severely depleted, compounding the already acute skills shortage.

| Generation | EU-27 employed | Primary zone exposure | Core risk mechanism | Adaptive capacity |
|-----------|---------------|----------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Boomers | 15–20M | Zone A/C (retiring) | Knowledge loss + compounded displacement in Zone A | Low (mitigated by retirement proximity) |
| Gen X | 55–60M | Zone B (senior) | Sustained transformation over 15–20 years; declining training uptake | Moderate (experience, but rigidity) |
| Millennials | 60–65M | Zone B (mid-career) | Highest absolute AI exposure; role transformation | High (skills, networks, time) |
| Gen Z | 30–35M | Entry-level (blocked) | Structural exclusion from workforce ladder | High potential, low current access |

**Bottom line for D.4:** The generational pipeline is fracturing at every level simultaneously. Boomers are exiting with irreplaceable knowledge. Gen X is frozen in place with declining adaptability. Millennials face maximum AI exposure but have the tools to navigate it. Gen Z cannot get on the ladder at all. The most dangerous dynamic is not any single generation's crisis but the **compounding interaction**: cutting entry-level hiring (Gen Z) while transforming mid-level roles (Millennials) while failing to transfer knowledge from departing workers (Boomers) creates a workforce that is simultaneously shrinking, destabilising, and losing institutional memory.

## The convergence: Europe's labour markets face asymmetric disruption, not symmetric decline

The thesis that "Europe's binding constraint is not job creation but workforce availability" is **strongly supported** by the evidence, but requires qualification. It is true for Zone C and Zone D — the roughly **95–105 million** workers in healthcare, trades, construction, care, agriculture, and AI/tech face a future of acute and worsening shortage that AI cannot remedy. For these workers, Europe is running out of people before it runs out of work. But it is false for Zone A, where the **25–30 million** clerical and administrative workers face a surplus of both AI capability and human labour — displacement will outpace retirement by a significant margin, creating a pool of displaced workers who cannot easily move to where they are needed.

The fundamental structural challenge for 2025–2040 is managing this asymmetry across five dimensions simultaneously:

- **The substitution asymmetry**: AI strongest in Zone A (moderate retirement pressure), weakest in Zone C (severe retirement pressure), creating an 8–12 million gap by 2030 that grows to 24–32 million by 2040
- **The mobility asymmetry**: Cross-zone retraining from cognitive to physical work is unprecedented, faces a 3% annual switching rate ceiling, and collapses in effectiveness for workers over 50 — who are the fastest-growing segment
- **The immigration asymmetry**: Immigrants fill Zone C shortages (67% of construction, 50%+ of food service, 30% of care) but political tolerance is declining as AfD polls at 25%+, FPÖ wins 28.8%, and SVP pushes population caps
- **The generational asymmetry**: Entry-level hiring collapses 73% for Gen Z while 40 million 55+ workers approach retirement, simultaneously depleting both ends of the talent pipeline
- **The geographic asymmetry**: Historical evidence shows displacement scarring lasts 30–50 years, meaning the spatial distribution of AI displacement (concentrated in office-heavy urban centres) will not naturally match the spatial distribution of Zone C shortages (distributed across hospitals, construction sites, care homes, and farms)

What makes this convergence historically unprecedented is not any single dimension — Europe has weathered demographic transitions, technological disruption, and migration pressures before — but their **simultaneity and misalignment**. Previous technological transitions were generational (young people chose new careers; old workers retired), took decades, and generally moved workers toward better conditions. The AI transition moves faster, hits mid-career workers hardest, and asks displaced knowledge workers to accept physically demanding, lower-status work. The demographic transition, meanwhile, removes the generational buffer that made past adjustments possible: there are simply fewer young workers entering the pipeline to organically fill new roles.

The policy implications are stark. Europe cannot rely on any single mechanism. AI will not fill Zone C gaps. Retraining cannot move millions of clerical workers into construction and care. Immigration meets economic needs but faces hardening political limits. The most realistic path involves **all four mechanisms operating at partial capacity**: AI absorbing Zone A retirement and boosting Zone B productivity; targeted retraining moving younger displaced workers into healthcare and care coordination; sustained immigration of ~400,000+/year to Germany alone despite political friction; and aggressive investment in robotics, assistive technology, and work redesign to extend the productive years of an ageing Zone C workforce. Even optimistically, a significant workforce gap — likely **5–10 million across the EU by 2030** — will persist. The question is not whether Europe faces a labour shortage, but how large it will be and whether institutions adapt fast enough to prevent it from becoming a crisis of care, infrastructure, and economic capacity.
