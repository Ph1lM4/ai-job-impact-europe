# **Quantitative Foundations of European Workforce Demographics: Structural Shifts, Migration Dependencies, and Long-Term Projections**

## **PART A — CURRENT POPULATION DATA**

The demographic architecture of the European Union and its closely integrated neighbours has entered a paradigm characterised by structural ageing, deeply entrenched sub-replacement fertility, and an absolute reliance on net migration for population maintenance. The data extracted from Eurostat, the United Nations Population Division, and OECD databases reveals a continent undergoing a profound transition, the macroeconomic implications of which are already materialising across labour markets and fiscal frameworks.

### **A.1 Total Population Dynamics and the Supremacy of Net Migration**

The aggregate population of the European Union has exhibited a highly volatile growth trajectory over the past half-decade, heavily distorted by the mortality shocks of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021) and the unprecedented migratory influx following the geopolitical destabilisation in Eastern Europe (2022–present).1 As of 1 January 2025, the estimated population of the EU-27 stands at 450.4 million inhabitants, representing a historical zenith.1 However, this aggregate growth obscures a fundamental structural reality: the gap between live births and deaths has widened to a point where organic population replenishment is a mathematical impossibility for the aggregate bloc.1

In 2024, the EU recorded 4.82 million deaths against only 3.56 million live births, resulting in a natural decrease of approximately 1.3 million individuals.1 Consequently, the 1.1 million absolute growth in the EU population in 2024 was driven exclusively by a net migration surplus of \+2.3 million.1

### **A.2 The Contraction of the Working-Age Population (20–64)**

While total population figures indicate stagnation offset by migration, the core working-age population (defined strictly as individuals aged 20–64) reveals a severe structural deficit. The macroeconomic implications of this contraction cannot be overstated; labour input serves as a primary variable in the Solow growth model, meaning absolute declines in the workforce place a hard ceiling on potential economic output.6

The European Union passed an invisible but monumental inflection point in 2009 when the EU-27 working-age population peaked at approximately 272 million individuals.7 Since that zenith, the continent has suffered a systemic hollowing out of its domestic labour supply, an effect macroeconomic literature refers to as a "demographic dividend in reverse".8

The causal mechanism dictating this contraction is firmly rooted in the retirement schedule of the baby-boomer generation (born between 1946 and 1964). As these outsized cohorts cross the 65-year threshold, the significantly smaller cohorts born during the low-fertility eras of the 1980s, 1990s, and 2000s are mathematically incapable of replacing them on a one-to-one basis.12 Germany illustrates this vulnerability acutely: for every 100 individuals aged 60–64 exiting the labour market, there are only roughly 82 individuals aged 20–24 entering it.13 Without mass migration, the German working-age base shrinks automatically each year. Conversely, Switzerland continues to post peak working-age numbers annually, a feat achieved strictly through targeted, high-volume economic migration from neighbouring EU states, effectively importing its workforce rather than breeding it.14

### **A.3 Age Structure and the Expansion of the "Very Old"**

To comprehend the qualitative strain this transition places on European economies, one must examine the specific age structure bands. The traditional demographic pyramid of the 20th century has transformed into a top-heavy, pillar-like structure.16 The median age in the EU expanded by over two full years between 2015 and 2025, reaching 44.9 years.17 Italy currently bears the highest median age in the bloc at 49.1 years, reflecting an acute deficit of youth paired with extended longevity.17

The most statistically aggressive shift within these bands is the rapid expansion of the 85+ cohort, designated the "very old." In the EU-27, the absolute number of individuals in this bracket is projected to more than double between 2019 and 2050\.16 This represents a severe fiscal liability. The per-capita expenditure required for Long-Term Care (LTC) and complex healthcare for an 85-year-old dwarfs the expenditure required for a 65-year-old.12 Because the 0–14 cohort continues to wither (dropping to 14.4% of the EU population in 2025), the future tax base responsible for funding this expanding care infrastructure is fundamentally compromised.20

### **A.4 The Collapse of Fertility Rates**

The root cause of organic population decline across Europe is entrenched, sub-replacement fertility. A total fertility rate (TFR) of 2.1 live births per woman is the standard benchmark required to maintain a stable population size absent of migration.21 Currently, no member state in the European Union meets or closely approximates this threshold.

In 2024, the EU-27 average TFR fell to an unprecedented historical low of 1.34 live births per woman, a steep deceleration from the recent peak of 1.57 recorded in 2008 and 2010\.21

**The Sub-1.3 Collapse:** The most alarming trends are observed in Southern Europe and specific Nordic states. Spain (1.10) and Italy (1.21) are experiencing profound fertility collapses.21 When a society falls below a TFR of 1.3 (often termed "lowest-low fertility"), the population base essentially halves roughly every 45 years absent migration. This creates a vicious demographic trap: drastically smaller youth cohorts today guarantee fewer potential mothers in the next generation, embedding contraction deeply into the population pyramid. It is notable that Finland, historically a stable Nordic welfare model, has also plummeted to a TFR of 1.26.23

**Tempo Effects and the French Anomaly:** A secondary mechanism influencing these statistics is the "tempo effect." The mean age of women at childbirth in the EU has risen consistently, reaching 31.3 years overall, and 29.9 years for the birth of a first child.21 While a fraction of the TFR depression is due to the delay of childbearing rather than absolute foregone births, tempo-adjusted fertility measures confirm that actual completed cohort fertility is still definitively shrinking across the continent.25

France remains the distinct outlier within Western Europe, maintaining a TFR of 1.61.21 France's relative resilience is broadly attributed to deeply institutionalised pro-natalist policies, robust state-subsidised early childcare infrastructure, and a social environment that minimises the friction between full-time female labour force participation and motherhood.26 Furthermore, immigrant populations are disproportionately propping up the aggregate fertility statistics across the bloc; in 2024, 24% of children born in the EU were to foreign-born mothers.21

**The Ukraine Displacement Effect (2022–Present):** The Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 generated a massive, asymmetrical demographic shock. By mid-2024, over 4.2 million non-EU citizens fleeing Ukraine held temporary protection status within the bloc.34

This influx artificially and rapidly inflated the working-age population metric in 2022 and 2023\. Unlike traditional asylum waves (which historically skew toward young males), the Ukrainian displacement is predominantly female, prime working-age (25–64), and highly educated, often accompanied by dependents.34 However, the translation of this raw demographic volume into actual full-time equivalent (FTE) labour output has experienced severe friction. Labour market integration has been bottlenecked by language acquisition requirements, an acute lack of childcare (as many Ukrainian women migrated as single parents), and bureaucratic hurdles in credential recognition.34 Consequently, while the working-age denominator swelled, the effective labour supply increased at a much slower rate.

### **A.6 Labour Force Participation and the "Inactive Reserve"**

To counteract the shrinking baseline demographic, European policymakers have executed strategies to maximise the labour force participation of the resident population. This approach has yielded remarkable statistical success. In 2024, the employment rate for individuals aged 20–64 in the EU reached a historical high of 75.8% (representing 197.6 million employed individuals), moving steadily toward the European Pillar of Social Rights target of 78% by 2030\.37

Despite these aggregate triumphs, the data reveals structural limits and unutilised reserves:

**The Retention of Older Workers (55–64 and 65+):** The single greatest driver of employment growth over the last decade has been the delayed retirement of older workers. The employment rate for the 55–64 bracket surged to 71.4% for men and 59.4% for women in 2024\.38 However, this lever has an absolute biological and legislative limit. Once this cohort crosses the 65+ threshold, participation plummets. This well of "easy" labour activation is approaching maximum capacity in states like Germany and Sweden, meaning future employment growth cannot rely on simply keeping older cohorts in the workforce longer.38

**The Gender Gap:** The employment rate for men stood at 80.8% compared to 70.8% for women, maintaining a stubborn gender employment gap of 10.0 percentage points.38 This gap widens significantly with age and lower educational attainment.38 The barriers here are structural: women disproportionately bear the burden of unpaid care work, lack access to affordable childcare, and face tax disincentives for second earners.37

**NEET Rates and the Inactive Reserve:** The share of young people Not in Education, Employment, or Training (NEET, aged 15–29) stood at 11.0% across the EU in 2024\.37 Variations are stark: the Netherlands boasts rates below 5%, Germany (8.5%) and Austria (9.2%) perform reasonably well, while regions in Southern Italy (Calabria at 26.2%) and peripheral Spain suffer endemic NEET rates, representing a critical failure to integrate prime-age youth.37

Simultaneously, the size of the "inactive reserve"—people of core working age (20–64) classified as outside the labour force—was 51.1 million in 2024\.11 This reserve comprises students, early retirees, individuals with long-term sickness, and those fulfilling unpaid care duties.11 While activating this reserve is frequently touted as a domestic alternative to immigration, the demographic reality suggests otherwise. A large portion of this reserve is structurally embedded in informal caregiving roles—a demand that will explicitly *increase* as the 85+ population swells and state-sponsored Long-Term Care systems become overwhelmed.12

## **PART B — PROJECTIONS**

### **B.1 Total Population Projections: The Spectre of Decline**

Long-term demographic forecasting relies on the cohort-component method. Eurostat’s EUROPOP2023 baseline projections are deterministic "what-if" models assuming a partial convergence of fertility, mortality, and migration trends across the bloc.41 Under these baseline assumptions, the EU population is expected to peak at 453.3 million in 2026 before entering a protracted period of decay, falling to 447.9 million by 2050, and ultimately sliding to 419.5 million by 2100\.41

However, baseline models assume consistent, heavy inward migration indefinitely. To isolate the true organic demographic momentum, one must evaluate the "No-Migration" sensitivity scenario.43 Under a zero net-migration environment, the contraction is existential.

### **B.2 The Implosion of the Working-Age Population (20–64)**

The most severe macroeconomic threat embedded in these projections is the accelerated implosion of the working-age demographic. While total populations may stagnate or grow slightly due to longevity, the 20–64 cohort is plunging.

The historical peak of 272 million workers in the EU-27 (2009) degraded to roughly 265 million by 2022\. According to baseline projections, this will drop to 258 million by 2030, 247 million by 2040, and 236 million by 2050\.7 This validates the widely cited macroeconomic estimate that the European Union is slated to lose approximately 1 million workers per year over the next quarter-century.45

Germany faces a precipitous drop, necessitating an impossible-to-sustain influx of 400,000+ net migrants *annually* just to keep the labour supply flat.46

### **B.3 The Old-Age Dependency Ratio (OADR)**

The Old-Age Dependency Ratio (OADR)—calculated as the number of individuals aged 65 and over per 100 people of working age (20–64)—is the primary metric dictating the sustainability of Pay-As-You-Go (PAYG) pension systems and public healthcare financing.47

The claim that the ratio will shift from "1 retiree per 3 workers to 1 per 2 by 2040" is analytically accurate. In 2022, there were approximately 2.7 workers per elderly person in the EU (an OADR of \~36%). By 2050, the OADR is projected to reach 50.4% (effectively 1 retiree per 2 workers), and by 2100, it is projected to climb to 65% (1.5 workers per retiree).12

This structural realignment fundamentally breaks the arithmetic of the post-war welfare state. In PAYG systems, the current workforce directly funds current retirees. As the ratio compresses toward 50%, the mathematical options become draconian: either taxation on the shrinking workforce must become economically punitive, pension benefits must be drastically curtailed, or the statutory retirement age must be aggressively indexed to life expectancy.49

### **B.4 Country Divergence Classification**

The European Union does not face a monolithic demographic destiny; rather, it faces a highly fragmented future shaped by the intersection of fertility floors, emigration drains, and immigration magnetism. The Member States can be classified into three distinct trajectories:

**Sharpest Decline (Population collapse \>30% by 2100 without migration)** This category includes Italy, Spain, Poland, Greece, the Baltics, and Romania. The mechanisms driving this decline are twofold. Southern Europe (Italy, Spain, Greece) is trapped in a "lowest-low" fertility cycle (TFR 1.1–1.2).23 Even with baseline migration, Italy loses 6 million workers by 2050\.7 Without migration, Italy and Spain face an absolute population reduction of roughly 49–52% by the end of the century.6 Eastern Europe (Poland, Romania, Baltics) suffers a double penalty: deeply depressed fertility compounded by decades of outward emigration of their prime working-age youth to Western Europe.51

**Moderate Decline (Heavy migration dependency)** This category encompasses Germany, Austria, and the Netherlands. These nations exhibit slightly higher fertility (1.3–1.4) than the South but still remain far below replacement.23 Their populations and workforces are currently propped up entirely by massive net inward migration.4 If Germany fails to integrate hundreds of thousands of migrants annually, its working-age cohort will contract violently.13 The Netherlands maintains growth strictly via positive migration balances, though immense geographical density and housing constraints threaten the political sustainability of this model.4

**Relative Resilience (Organic stabilization)** This category includes France, Ireland, and Sweden. France possesses a uniquely resilient demographic profile. With a TFR of 1.61, it operates much closer to replacement levels than its peers, shielding its future working-age base from severe hollowing.23 Ireland benefits from historically high fertility (only recently tapering) and a highly attractive migration profile for multinational tech/pharma workers.23 Sweden combines robust state-funded family policies promoting work-life balance with high levels of immigration, allowing it to project a stabilised working-age population through 2040\.15

### **B.5 Macroeconomic and Fiscal Impacts: The Demographic Dividend in Reverse**

The macroeconomic consequences of this demographic transition are starkly outlined in the European Commission’s 2024 Ageing Report.53 The "demographic dividend"—the mid-20th-century period when a disproportionately large share of the population was of working age, driving savings, investment, and GDP growth—is now operating in reverse.8

Because labour input is a primary function of the Solow growth model, the absolute decline in the number of workers applies a hard physical limit on output. The EU annual GDP growth is forecast to average a sluggish 1.3% from 2022 to 2070, dragged down inherently by demographic friction.6 Even assuming total factor productivity (TFP) grows optimally (projected to rise to 1.2% by 2040), it cannot entirely offset the loss of tens of millions of workers.6 The capital stock per worker may rise, but aggregate demand will inevitably shift away from durable goods and housing toward services and healthcare.6

The fiscal burden of the 85+ cohort expansion is severe. The 2024 Ageing Report projects that public healthcare expenditure in the EU will increase from 6.9% of GDP in 2022 to 7.3% by 2070\.12 More critically, Long-Term Care (LTC) spending—which is highly correlated with the 85+ demographic—is projected to rise by 0.8 percentage points, from 1.7% to 2.6% of GDP.12

Combined with compounding pension obligations, these rising age-related expenditures will crowd out public investment in infrastructure, education, and the green/digital transitions. The resulting pension sustainability pressure dictates that without comprehensive reform—such as linking the statutory retirement age to life expectancy and radically expanding the complementary funded (private) pension pillars—the sovereign debt profiles of heavily aged nations (notably Italy and Spain) will face intense destabilisation in the coming decades.49
