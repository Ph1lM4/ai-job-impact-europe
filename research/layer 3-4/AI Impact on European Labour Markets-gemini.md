# **Architecting a 6-Layer Analytical Framework on Artificial Intelligence and European Labour Market Dynamics: Comprehensive Data Infrastructure and Source Repository**

## **Executive Synthesis of the Analytical Architecture**

The intersection of artificial intelligence integration and European demographic contraction presents an unprecedented macroeconomic vector. Constructing a robust six-layer analytical framework to quantify this phenomenon requires an exhaustive, rigorously cross-validated data infrastructure. Standard aggregations and top-line summaries are insufficient for predictive modeling; true structural analysis demands granular microdata, task-level taxonomies, and precise longitudinal tracking of historical analogues.

The framework must mathematically synthesize six distinct domains: (1) fundamental demographic and labour supply trajectories, accounting for severe cohort replacement deficits; (2) existing macroeconomic frictions, specifically quantifying structural job vacancies and localized skill shortages; (3) algorithmic task exposure, decomposing ISCO-08 occupational codes into discrete, analyzable O\*NET task statements; (4) historical transition costs, calibrating predictive models against the empirical realities of past General Purpose Technologies (GPTs); (5) institutional forecasts, measuring the divergence between theoretical technological capabilities and ground-truth corporate capital expenditure; and (6) dynamic data visualization environments to continuously test multidimensional scenarios.

This report establishes the definitive data repository required to operationalize this architecture. It provides the exact, ingestible data streams, academic literature, institutional primary surveys, and theoretical models necessary to construct a resilient, predictive labour economic framework.

**CATEGORY 1: Downloadable Datasets and Databases**

The foundational layer of the framework relies on raw, ingestible statistical matrices. Tracking the precise occupational footprints of an aging European workforce against inbound automation technologies requires sophisticated cross-tabulations of employment structures. The European Union Labour Force Survey (EU-LFS) is the central pillar here. While public tables (such as lfsa\_egais and lfsa\_egai2d) offer aggregated insights at the 1-digit and 2-digit International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO-08) levels, they are insufficient for granular retirement-by-occupation projections due to top-coding and age band aggregation. For precise lifecycle modeling, access to the EU-LFS Scientific Use Files (SUFs) is mandatory. Within these microdata sets, researchers must navigate perturbation protocols, where the variable AGE is frequently collapsed into 5-year bands (AGE\_GRP) and spatial resolution is limited to NUTS 2 regions.1

Simultaneously, evaluating structural labour deficits requires high-resolution job vacancy statistics. Eurostat’s jvs\_q\_isco\_r2 dataset provides unadjusted quarterly data on vacancies by NACE Rev. 2 activity and ISCO-08 occupation.2 However, this data frequently suffers from sparsity and methodological breaks at the national level.3 To fortify this layer, localized administrative data must be integrated. The German Federal Employment Agency (Bundesagentur für Arbeit) provides the definitive *Fachkräfteengpassanalyse*, a highly structured dataset detailing 163 specific occupations facing acute shortages.4 The Austrian Public Employment Service (AMS) mirrors this with its *Arbeitsmarktdaten* and *Fachkräftebarometer*, tracking 81 persistent bottleneck occupations.5

To map these macroeconomic trends to AI capabilities, the framework must utilize the US Department of Labor’s O*NET database (Version 28.2). O*NET decomposes occupations into thousands of specific task statements and work activities.7 Because European employment data utilizes ISCO-08, researchers must apply ILO crosswalk tables to translate O\*NET's Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) architecture into the European context.9 This crosswalking is the critical mechanism that allows US-developed algorithmic exposure indices—such as the AI Occupational Exposure (AIOE) index by Felten, Raj, and Seamans—to be projected onto the European labour supply.11

### **Methodological Integration into the Framework**

To execute the baseline projection, the framework must join the O\*NET task statements to the ISCO-08 crosswalk, calculate an exposure coefficient for each ISCO code based on the AIOE methodology, and multiply that coefficient by the active worker count derived from the EU-LFS microdata. This resultant "disruption volume" is then discounted against the projected retirement outflows sourced from the BFS and WorldPop demographic models, effectively revealing whether AI automation will outpace, match, or fall behind the rate of natural workforce attrition.

**CATEGORY 2: Academic Papers and Working Papers**

The theoretical scaffolding of the 6-layer framework relies on econometrics and empirical labour economics. The literature mapping is divided into three crucial vectors: the measurement of algorithmic exposure, the analysis of historical technological transition costs, and the dynamics of demographic supply constraint.

### **AI and Labour Market Exposure**

The methodology for quantifying exposure has evolved rapidly. Early frameworks, such as Frey and Osborne’s 2013/2017 paper, relied on probabilistic assessments of broad occupational classifications, leading to the infamous—and largely overestimated—conclusion that 47% of US employment was susceptible to computerisation. Modern methodologies reject this monolithic approach. Webb (2020) pioneered the use of natural language processing to match the verb-noun pairs in patent filings to specific occupational tasks, successfully differentiating the distinct historical footprints of software, robotics, and artificial intelligence.

Building on this, Eloundou et al. (2023) developed the baseline framework for Large Language Model (LLM) exposure, finding that roughly 80% of the workforce could see at least 10% of their tasks affected by Generative Pre-trained Transformers. However, exposure does not guarantee displacement. Georgieff and Milanez (2021), utilizing OECD data, empirically demonstrated that occupations historically flagged as highly exposed to automation actually experienced *employment growth*, highlighting the pervasive effect of capital-labour complementarity.12 This complementarity is further observed by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2023), whose empirical study of generative AI in customer service contexts revealed a 14% productivity increase driven primarily by the rapid upskilling of novice workers via tacit knowledge transfer. Most critically, Acemoglu (2024) applies hard macroeconomic constraints to these micro-level productivity gains, mathematically demonstrating that the Jevons paradox and systemic integration bottlenecks will likely restrict AI-driven Total Factor Productivity (TFP) growth to a mere 0.53% over the next decade.

### **Historical Technology Disruptions**

Predictive models that assume seamless technological transitions consistently fail. The framework must integrate frictional transition costs observed during historical GPT adoptions. Paul David’s seminal 1990 paper on the dynamo and the computer maps the "productivity paradox," proving that macro-level economic gains require decades of total systemic restructuring before manifesting in aggregate data. Feigenbaum and Gross (2024) provide the definitive micro-level empirical analysis of displacement friction, detailing the severe, permanent wage penalties suffered by telephone operators automated out by mechanical switching arrays in the early 20th century.

Conversely, Bessen’s work on ATMs demonstrates how automation can lower operational costs to the extent that product demand becomes highly elastic, paradoxically driving an *increase* in the number of bank tellers employed. The spatial and regional distribution of these shocks must be modeled using the methodology outlined by Autor, Dorn, and Hanson in their "China Shock" papers, which track how localized labour markets fail to clear and reallocate workers efficiently following an exogenous disruption. Finally, European specificities are captured by Dauth et al. (2017), who demonstrated that unlike the US experience (mapped by Acemoglu & Restrepo), the massive adoption of industrial robots in Germany altered the task composition of the manufacturing sector but did *not* result in net aggregate employment losses.

### **Demographics and Labour Supply**

The technological shock of AI is colliding with a profound demographic vacuum. As documented by the IW Köln and highlighted in the Bruegel policy briefs, the German labour market is entering an era where only 82 entrants replace every 100 retirees. The ECB’s 2024/2026 economic bulletins confirm that rather than acting as a displacement force, AI is currently correlated with *increased* hiring within highly exposed European sectors, suggesting that firms are aggressively hoarding talent to manage the digital transition amidst a shrinking talent pool.

**CATEGORY 3: Reports and White Papers (2024-2026)**

Theoretical macro-models assume a level of enterprise agility that rarely exists in practice. To accurately constrain AI disruption timelines, the framework must continuously ingest institutional survey data tracking actual corporate capital expenditure, integration timelines, and realized skill gaps.

The World Economic Forum's (WEF) Future of Jobs Report provides irreplaceable ground-truth data sourced directly from Chief Human Resources Officers (CHROs) of global enterprises. The 2025 data systematically reveals that enterprise leadership anticipates net job creation through technology integration over the next five years, prioritizing upskilling over aggressive headcount reduction. Similarly, the ILO's World Employment and Social Outlook monitors the degradation of job quality and the emergence of non-standard employment contracts, noting that AI currently acts primarily to augment, rather than replace, administrative architectures.

The OECD Employment Outlook offers authoritative, cross-national primary data on enterprise AI adoption rates. Recent iterations emphasize that despite extreme exposure metrics, actual operational deployment of generative AI across European SMEs remains deeply suppressed by regulatory caution (e.g., the EU AI Act), data privacy concerns, and severe shortages of specialized integration engineering talent. Private sector surveys from ManpowerGroup and Korn Ferry quantify this exact shortage, projecting multi-trillion-dollar macroeconomic deficits caused not by an excess of automation, but by a catastrophic lack of the human talent required to build, maintain, and supervise these automated systems.

**CATEGORY 4: Books Worth Extracting**

To construct a predictive algorithm, qualitative logic and historical constraints must be extracted from long-form economic literature and converted into rules-based framework logic. These texts provide the mental models that dictate how the framework weighs competing variables.

Robert Gordon’s "The Rise and Fall of American Growth" is structurally vital. Extracting Gordon's stagnation thesis allows the framework to build a robust counter-weight to exponential AI hype, demanding that any projected technological productivity boom account for the compounding headwinds of demographics, inequality, and sovereign debt. Carl Benedikt Frey’s "The Technology Trap" must be extracted to operationalize the distinction between "enabling" technologies (which increase worker productivity and wage bargaining power) and "replacing" technologies (which strictly substitute capital for labour).

Daniel Susskind's "A World Without Work" introduces the "pragmatic fallacy," altering the framework's assessment parameters by confirming that machine learning architectures do not need to replicate human cognitive processes to achieve identical economic outputs, thereby vastly expanding the radius of automatable non-routine tasks. Finally, Aaron Benanav’s "Automation and the Future of Work" offers a critical Marxist-economic counter-narrative: the framework must test his hypothesis that global underemployment is not the result of brilliant automated technologies, but rather a symptom of chronic industrial overcapacity and macroeconomic stagnation.

*(Note on David Autor: Autor is one of the most consequential labour economists alive, fundamentally architecting the ALM task-based model and the China Shock framework. However, his work is disseminated almost entirely through peer-reviewed journal articles and NBER working papers rather than standalone commercial books. Therefore, his models are thoroughly captured in Category 2, and extracting a specific "book" is unnecessary.)*
