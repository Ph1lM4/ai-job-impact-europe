# What actually happened to displaced workers: eight cases, one pattern

**The historical record delivers a blunt verdict: most workers displaced by major technological disruptions did not successfully retrain, did not relocate, and did not recover their prior incomes.** Across two centuries of evidence — from hand-loom weavers to retail workers — the exposed population divides into a minority who transition (disproportionately young, educated, and in growing economies) and a majority who experience lasting income decline, labour-force exit via disability systems, and community-level scarring that persists for decades. The consistency of this pattern across radically different technologies, time periods, and institutional contexts constitutes the most important finding. Government retraining programmes, evaluated rigorously, show modest-to-null average effects on earnings — the most comprehensive US evaluation found participants earned **$3,300 less** than non-participants four years later. Prevention of displacement (the German Kurzarbeit model) dramatically outperforms attempted cure.

## Case 1: Hand-loom weavers endured a 60-year death spiral

The displacement of Britain's hand-loom weavers by power looms (c. 1800–1860s) is the longest and most devastating case in the record. At peak around 1812–1834, there were **240,000–400,000 hand-loom weavers** in Britain. Their wages collapsed from roughly **20 shillings per week in 1806 to 5 shillings by the late 1820s** — a 75% decline in real income — yet their numbers actually *increased* during this period because no alternative occupations existed. This is a critical finding: workers remained trapped in a declining occupation for decades precisely because they had nowhere else to go.

E.P. Thompson argued that most hand-loom weavers "left the trade simply because they died off, either of old age, or prematurely as a consequence of their reduced economic circumstances." Duncan Bythell's definitive 1969 study confirmed that until the great expansion of factory work from the 1820s onward, movement out of hand-loom weaving was extremely limited. Working conditions deteriorated grotesquely — weavers who once worked four days a week earning 40 shillings were by the 1830s working **14–16 hours a day, six days a week** for a quarter of the pay. Parliamentary investigations (the 1837–1841 Royal Commission) documented communities where weavers had sold all household goods to avoid starvation.

Community-level devastation was severe. Lancashire weaving towns like Padiham and Burnley had poor-relief costs **1.7 to 2 times higher** than towns with fewer hand-loom weavers. In east Lancashire, an estimated **40% of the population** depended on hand-loom weaving. Generational escape occurred not through retraining of the weavers themselves but through their children avoiding the trade and entering factories — a pattern of **generational replacement rather than worker transition** that recurs across subsequent cases. The full displacement took approximately **40–60 years**, and Acemoglu and Johnson (2024) estimate that economy-wide living standards did not meaningfully improve until the 1840s–1850s — "an average working lifetime" of immiseration.

## Case 2: Dockworkers were protected by institutions but their communities were not

Containerisation eliminated **75–90% of dock employment** globally between the 1960s and 1980s. The UK's Registered Dock Worker population fell from **81,000 in 1955 to under 10,000 at the National Dock Labour Scheme's abolition in 1989** — an 88% decline. The US Port of New York/New Jersey workforce plummeted from **55,000 in the 1950s to roughly 4,000** today. Productivity gains were staggering: tonnage moved per worker jumped from **1.5 to 37.5 tons per hour**, a 25-fold increase.

The critical empirical study — El-Sahli and Upward's 2017 analysis in the *British Journal of Industrial Relations* using ONS Longitudinal Study data — found that individual dock workers' labour-market outcomes were "rather small, insignificantly different from zero." But this finding carries a devastating caveat: dock workers fared no worse than comparable unskilled men because **the entire unskilled working class experienced terrible outcomes** during this period. The NDLS insulated dock workers for two decades through voluntary redundancy with generous severance (£25,000–£35,000 payoffs), but the district-level data tells a starkly different story. Port districts that contained major ports in 1971 had **significantly lower employment than non-port districts by 1991, a gap that persisted for 40 years** through 2011.

Three institutional models produced different outcomes. The ILWU's 1960 Mechanization and Modernization Agreement on the US West Coast traded work rules for no-layoff guarantees and financial packages — creating a small "labour aristocracy" (current ILWU "A" workers average **$171,000/year**) at the cost of closing the door on new entrants. The ILA on the East Coast negotiated a per-ton "container royalty" but still saw **73% workforce decline** (40,000 to ~11,000). Both US unions cushioned existing workers but could not prevent massive aggregate job loss.

London Docklands illustrates the regeneration paradox. After dock closures left 60% of the area derelict and unemployment reached **17.8%**, the LDDC invested **£1.86 billion**, creating 120,000 new jobs. But most new employment was in financial services inaccessible to former dockers. The original community experienced what Tim Butler (2007) called "a very clear-cut case of regeneration as gentrification" — luxury developments alongside run-down estates, with the original population marginalised and displaced. Liverpool dock communities experienced **46% unemployment** in the early 1980s, alongside heroin epidemics and the Toxteth riots.

## Case 3: The China Shock proved displaced workers simply do not move

The displacement of US manufacturing workers (1980s–2010s) is the most rigorously studied case in the literature. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson's "China Shock" research represents the gold standard: they found that Chinese import competition accounted for **59.3% of all US manufacturing job losses between 2001 and 2019**, destroying an estimated **2.0–2.4 million jobs** including spillover effects. The total US manufacturing workforce fell from **19.5 million in 1979 to 12.8 million by 2019** — a 35% decline.

The most striking finding is geographic immobility. Manufacturing job losses converted **"nearly one-for-one into long-term unemployment"** — workers did not relocate to areas with better prospects, did not shift into non-manufacturing employment, and did not leave the labour force through normal channels. Instead, the system of adjustment was disability insurance. Per-capita SSDI payments in trade-exposed regions were **30–40 times larger** than Trade Adjustment Assistance payments. TAA is temporary; most SSDI recipients receive benefits until retirement or death. The affected commuting zones showed persistent adverse effects through **2019 — nearly two decades after the initial shock**.

Earnings-loss research paints an equally grim picture. Jacobson, LaLonde, and Sullivan's foundational 1993 study found high-tenure displaced workers suffered **25% annual earnings losses** with little evidence of recovery even after three years. Davis and von Wachter (2011), using Social Security data nationally, found workers displaced during recessions suffered lifetime earnings losses of roughly **$112,100 over 25 years** — with earnings still **15–20% below** expected levels two decades later. Autor et al. (2014) found that a worker at the 75th versus 25th percentile of trade exposure lost cumulative earnings equal to **56% of initial yearly income** over 15 years.

The mortality connection, documented by Case and Deaton, is devastating. "Deaths of despair" — drug overdoses, alcohol-related liver disease, and suicide — rose sharply in deindustrialised communities. Drug overdose mortality rose **more than fourfold** from 1999 to 2013 among middle-aged white Americans. Pierce and Schott (2020) directly linked trade liberalisation to fatal drug overdoses, finding that moving a county from the 25th to 75th percentile of trade exposure increased overdose mortality by **40–60%**. An estimated **500,000 excess deaths of despair** occurred among middle-aged white non-Hispanic Americans since 1998. Sullivan and von Wachter (2009) found displacement itself causes a **1.0–1.5 year reduction in life expectancy**, with mortality rates **50–100% higher** in the year following job loss.

Rust Belt cities provide the community-level evidence. Detroit's population fell **60%** from its peak of 1.85 million; it filed for bankruptcy in 2013. Gary, Indiana lost over **50%** of its population and has **13,000 vacant structures**. Youngstown lost **50,000 jobs within five years** of its first steel closure in 1977 and now has nearly **40% poverty**. Pittsburgh stands as the sole major success story — its pre-existing university infrastructure (Carnegie Mellon, University of Pittsburgh) enabled a pivot to healthcare and technology — but this exception proves the rule about human capital as the determinant of recovery.

## Case 4: Print compositors experienced craft destruction without systematic tracking

The displacement of typesetters by desktop publishing (1985–2000) destroyed a well-paid, highly unionised craft occupation. The US International Typographical Union peaked at roughly **100,000 members in the 1960s** and ceased operations in 1986 with just **40,000 active members**. UK printing employment fell to just **9,900 skilled print workers** by 2017.

The Wapping dispute of 1986 was the watershed event. Rupert Murdoch secretly equipped a new plant with computerised typesetting, then engineered a strike to dismiss **5,500–6,000 workers** — deliberately timed to avoid statutory redundancy payments. The displaced workers had no rival newspapers to work for, as all Fleet Street papers subsequently adopted similar technology and left for east London. Baroness Brenda Dean, former SOGAT General Secretary, explicitly called for "a serious academic study" of what happened to these workers — a study that has **never been conducted**. This is a significant gap in the literature.

Where evidence exists, it shows mixed transitions. The New York Times's 1974 agreement guaranteed lifetime employment to **1,785 compositors** in exchange for accepting electronic composition — the best-case scenario, with the last lifetime-guaranteed worker retiring in 2016 after successfully transitioning to Mac-based InDesign work. The ITU noted that moving from TTS operators to IBM typewriters to desk computers was "an easy switch," but "after the onset of home computers, the internet, and desktop publishing, there was simply no work left for composing rooms." Cynthia Cockburn's interviews documented that the technology "wipes out many of the aspects of the work" that defined the craft, producing "subjective degradation" even among those who technically transitioned.

The compositor case highlights a recurring pattern: **strong unions could negotiate generous transitions when they cooperated (NYT) but were destroyed when they resisted (Wapping)**. The division between the NGA and SOGAT — maintaining a craft/non-craft split "beyond its useful life" — meant print unions were divided precisely when unity was essential.

## Case 5: Telephone operators benefited from decades of runway and a growing economy

Telephone operators offer the closest thing to a "good news" case — though with important caveats. The workforce peaked at over **342,000 telecom operators plus roughly one million private PBX operators** by 1950 in the US. Displacement took **57 years** from AT&T's first serious adoption of automatic switching (1919–1921) to full phase-out (1978). This extraordinarily gradual national timeline is the distinguishing feature.

Feigenbaum and Gross's 2024 study in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* — using complete-count census data linked to AT&T archives — found that **incumbent operators** in cities that automated suffered real costs: they were less likely to be working a decade later, and those who were working occupied lower-paying occupations. About **10% of displaced operators** ended up in lower-paying jobs compared to only **1% of non-exposed peers**. But the effects were "relatively modest" partly because many women would have exited the workforce anyway under prevailing social norms.

The critical finding concerns **future cohorts**: after a city automated, the number of young women employed as operators dropped **50–80%**, but overall female employment rates did not decline. Jobs were absorbed into clerical and service-sector work. However, Feigenbaum and Gross explicitly caution that this adjustment "relied on broader economic growth during the interwar period rather than seamless reallocation." The growing service economy, expanding female educational attainment, and multi-year advance notice of cutovers all facilitated transition — conditions that cannot be assumed in other contexts. In manufacturing-heavy cities or during the Depression, reinstatement of demand was slower and weaker.

## Case 6: Mining communities provide the strongest evidence of permanent scarring

The UK coal industry shed approximately **250,000 jobs** between the early 1980s and mid-2000s, from **237,000 workers in 1980** to fewer than 7,000 by 2005. The last deep mine (Kellingley, Yorkshire) closed in December 2015. Christina Beatty and Steve Fothergill's research programme at Sheffield Hallam University, spanning three decades, constitutes the most sustained longitudinal study of displacement community outcomes anywhere in the world.

Their foundational finding overturned official statistics: **the main consequence of mining job loss was not registered unemployment but diversion onto incapacity/disability benefits**. Claimant unemployment in coalfields remained no higher than when mines were working — masking the true scale of joblessness. Over **90,000 men** in the English and Welsh coalfields were diverted from unemployment onto incapacity benefits, nearly double the number of male claimant unemployed. This "hidden unemployment" mechanism has been replicated in every subsequent UK study of industrial displacement.

Forty years later, the data shows no full recovery. The 2024 State of the Coalfields report found only **57 employee jobs per 100 working-age residents** in former coalfields versus **73 nationally**. Median hourly earnings remain **8–10% below** the national average. One in six adults of working age claims out-of-work benefits. Life expectancy is approximately **one year below** the national average and **three years below** South East England. A 2025 study by Saville and Williams found deaths of despair remain **consistently higher** in former coal communities even after controlling for deprivation. Grimethorpe in Yorkshire, where unemployment hit **50%** after its colliery closed in 1992, was named the poorest village in England in a 1994 EU report.

US Appalachian coal country shows a parallel trajectory compressed into a shorter timeframe. Coal employment fell from roughly **92,000 in 2011 to 41,600 in 2020** — with **87% of job losses concentrated in Appalachia**. Kentucky longitudinal data (KYSTATS, 2024) tracked workers separated from coal jobs between 2007 and 2017: **37% never held covered employment in Kentucky again**. Of those who did find work, only **28% were employed consistently** at one, three, and five-year horizons. Just over half ever earned comparable wages, taking on average **more than five years** to reach prior pay. Diseases of despair mortality in Appalachia runs **45–53% higher** than the non-Appalachian US, with opioid overdose rates **50% higher** and the 35–44 age group experiencing mortality **55–66% above** non-Appalachian rates. The 2008 Department of Labor Gold Standard evaluation of retraining programmes covering 160,000 laid-off workers concluded: "It appears possible that ultimate gains from participation are small or nonexistent."

Generational effects are pronounced. Oxford University oral history research found a gendered pattern: **daughters of miners tended to do better than sons**, often through mothers who returned to education. Bukodi and Goldthorpe (2019) found that relative rates of intergenerational social class mobility have remained essentially constant since WWII — with deindustrialisation actually producing **more downward mobility**. The UK Social Mobility Commission's 2025 report states explicitly: "Britons growing up today in former industrial and mining heartlands are still living with the impacts of deindustrialisation" — **half a century** of economic disadvantage.

## Case 7: Retail displacement is poorly tracked but the trajectory points downward

The retail displacement lacks the longitudinal academic attention of manufacturing or mining, but the scale is significant. US department stores shed **241,000 employees** in the five years to 2020. UK retail lost **nearly 170,000 jobs in 2024 alone** — a 42% increase from 2023, with ~13,500 stores permanently closing. Specific collapses were enormous: Debenhams (**12,000 jobs**), BHS (**11,000 jobs**), Arcadia (**13,000 jobs**), Sears (**175,000 jobs** over its decade of decline), Toys "R" Us (**30,000 jobs** in the US).

Where did these workers go? The evidence suggests a split between warehouse/logistics work, gig economy participation, other retail, and labour-force exit — but **no comprehensive longitudinal study tracking displaced retail workers exists**. The TIME magazine investigation emphasised that unlike manufacturing workers who had Trade Adjustment Assistance, retail workers have no equivalent support infrastructure — no "internet-affected worker" category.

The wage comparison is roughly lateral to slightly downward. US retail cashiers earn a median of **$13–15/hour**; Amazon warehouse associates start at **$15–17/hour** with an average of **$18.59**. This looks like a small improvement, but the National Employment Law Project found Amazon warehouse workers earn **26% less** than average local earnings and **18% less** than non-Amazon warehouse workers in comparable counties. Warehouse work is dramatically more physically demanding, with Amazon injury rates of **9.6 per 100 workers** — double the national average. Gig economy work is worse: over half of UK gig workers earn **less than minimum wage**, with a median annual gig income of just **£375**. The Resolution Foundation notes that over four-fifths of recent UK job losses have been in retail and hospitality, concentrated among the most vulnerable workers.

## Case 8: Bank tellers represent the best-case displacement scenario — and it is still mediocre

James Bessen's influential finding that ATMs initially *increased* teller employment — by lowering per-branch costs and enabling branch expansion — stopped being true precisely as it became widely cited. Mobile banking reversed the trend: US bank teller employment fell from **527,680 in 2013 to 347,400 by 2024**, a **30% decline**. JPMorgan Chase reported tellers handled only **42% of deposits in 2015**, down from **90% in 2007**. The UK has lost over **6,000 bank branches since 2015** — more than 60% of the banking network.

Banks pursued active internal redeployment through "Universal Banker" roles combining teller functions with advisory duties. The Burning Glass Institute's 2025 analysis found **64% of tellers are promoted within three years** — but most promotions are semi-lateral (teller to senior teller). Only **4% transition into genuinely higher-paying roles** like loan officers. The most lucrative transition (to Personal/Relationship Banker) yields up to **$18,000 more**, but these roles have seen **~25% wage erosion** since creation — from ~$70,000 to ~$52,000. The fundamental barrier is educational: **83% of tellers lack a bachelor's degree**, which gates most upward mobility paths within banking.

Bank tellers represent the most managed displacement in this study — gradual decline, active employer investment in retraining, some upward pathways. Yet even here, most workers experience only modest income gains, the replacement role is degrading in value, and the entry-level gateway function for non-degree workers is being permanently closed.

## The retraining myth has strong empirical support

The most rigorous evaluation of the US Trade Adjustment Assistance programme (Mathematica/D'Amico and Schochet, 2012) found that TAA participants earned approximately **$3,300 less** than matched comparison workers four years later. Roughly **60% of TAA participants enrolled in training**, about **80% of those completed it**, but only **37% found employment in their retrained field**. The pipeline from eligibility to retrained-field employment is thin: of every 100 eligible workers, roughly 60 train, 48 complete, and 18 get jobs in their new field.

The National JTPA Study — the gold-standard randomised controlled trial of US job training — found modest positive effects for adult women (~$1,300 over 30 months) and adult men (~$1,200), but **zero to negative effects for youth**. Male youth participants actually earned less than controls. The OECD's Card, Kluve, and Weber meta-analysis of **207 studies and 857 programme estimates** worldwide found short-run training impacts "near zero," with modest positive effects emerging only after **two or more years** — and only for certain subgroups (adult women, the long-term unemployed).

James Heckman's landmark review concluded: **"These programs usually have at best a modest impact on participants' labor market prospects"** and **"many programs and initiatives cannot pass a cost-benefit test."** His broader body of work established that returns to human capital investment decline steeply with age — adult retraining "cannot remedy the skill deficits accumulated over a lifetime." The UK Work Programme achieved job outcomes for only **28%** of 1.81 million referrals, with early results (3.6% sustained employment) actually *worse* than the no-intervention baseline of 5%.

The German Kurzarbeit model represents the most effective contrast. During the 2008–09 crisis, Germany's GDP collapsed by ~7% yet **unemployment rose less than one percentage point**. An estimated **400,000 jobs were saved** at a cost of ~€5–6 billion — versus the US, which spent **$115 billion on unemployment compensation** in 2009 alone, plus retraining programmes of demonstrably limited effectiveness. The core lesson: **preventing displacement vastly outperforms retraining after displacement**. Kurzarbeit preserves firm-specific human capital, avoids the scarring effects of unemployment, and maintains workers' attachment to employers. It is, however, most suited to temporary demand shocks — it cannot address permanent structural decline.

## Age 50 is where recovery collapses

The evidence across multiple displacement events, countries, and time periods converges on a consistent age-outcome gradient with a critical threshold around **age 50**. BLS Displaced Worker Surveys show reemployment rates of **~72–75% for ages 25–54** dropping to **~64–67% for ages 55–64** and plunging to **24–44% for 65+**. The Urban Institute found that displaced men ages 50–61 are **39% less likely** to become reemployed each month than those ages 25–34.

Earnings losses steepen dramatically with age. The OECD found that earnings of displaced workers aged 46–50 are **cut in half** relative to non-displaced counterparts five years later — losses approximately **five times as large** as those for workers aged 18–24. The Urban Institute found median household income fell **42%** after a post-50 involuntary job separation using Health and Retirement Study data. Workers aged 60+ suffer earnings losses of **34.4%** compared to 7.1% for those 50–59.

The data reveals a gradient rather than a single cliff. Around age **45–50**, outcomes begin deteriorating notably. By **55**, the decline is steep — reemployment drops 8–11 percentage points, long-term unemployment share rises 12 percentage points above younger workers, and disability pathways open. By **60+**, the system effectively collapses — most displaced workers never return to equivalent employment. Retraining can technically work for older workers in terms of skill acquisition (Jacobson et al. found similar learning rates), but the economic return is approximately **half** that for younger workers due to shorter remaining careers and foregone earnings during training.

## Geographic scarring may be permanent

The evidence on community-level recovery is the most disturbing finding in this entire literature. Edward Glaeser's research established that cities are remarkably resilient to physical destruction (bombings, earthquakes, fires) but **highly vulnerable to economic shocks**, especially deindustrialisation. The data suggests that some communities never recover.

UK coalfields, 40 years after pit closures, still have employment rates **more than 2 percentage points behind** the national average, would need **80,000 additional residents** in work to match national rates, and show **42% of neighbourhoods in the most deprived 30%** nationally. US Rust Belt cities tell a similar story over even longer timeframes: Detroit has lost 60% of its population over 70 years, Gary has 13,000 abandoned buildings, and Youngstown has 40% poverty. Hershbein and Stuart (2023) found that employment declines emerging during recessions are "extremely persistent," overturning the earlier Blanchard and Katz (1992) finding that local areas recover rapidly.

The single strongest predictor of whether a community recovers is **pre-existing human capital**. Pittsburgh's universities enabled reinvention; Detroit's low educational attainment (12% degree holders versus Pittsburgh's 31%) precluded it. Geographic isolation, economic monoculture, and selective out-migration of the young and educated create self-reinforcing decline. Beatty and Fothergill (2020) describe this as "the long shadow of job loss" — a shadow that, four decades on, shows no sign of lifting.

Intergenerational transmission compounds the damage. Oreopoulos, Page, and Stevens (2008) found children of displaced fathers earn **9% less** as adults, with effects concentrated when displacement occurs during childhood ages 10–14. Raj Chetty's work shows dramatic geographic variation in upward mobility that maps closely onto areas of concentrated displacement. The UK Social Mobility Commission identifies former industrial areas as "entrenched social mobility cold spots" where young people are **half as likely** to attend university as those in high-mobility areas.

## What the historical record predicts for AI displacement

Ten patterns emerge with striking consistency across all eight cases and 200 years of evidence. These patterns, not speculative forecasts, constitute the empirical basis for anticipating AI's labour-market effects.

**First, displaced workers overwhelmingly do not move.** This held for hand-loom weavers trapped in Lancashire, dock workers remaining in Liverpool, manufacturing workers staying in trade-exposed commuting zones, and coal miners in Appalachia. Geographic mobility has been *declining* for 40 years — the share of Americans moving annually has fallen from roughly 20% to 7.8%. There is no reason to expect AI-displaced workers to be more mobile.

**Second, retraining at scale does not work.** From TAA to JTPA to the UK Work Programme, rigorous evaluations consistently show modest-to-null earnings impacts. The pipeline from displacement to retrained employment is leaky at every stage. If AI displaces white-collar workers with higher baseline education, retraining may be somewhat more effective — but the meta-analytic evidence offers no basis for confidence in large-scale retraining as a solution.

**Third, age 50 is the approximate point of no return.** Across every displacement studied, workers over 50 face dramatically worse outcomes — halved earnings, 39% lower monthly reemployment probability, and high rates of permanent labour-force exit. AI displacement will disproportionately harm older knowledge workers who cannot practically retrain into new fields.

**Fourth, the actual adjustment mechanism is disability and labour-force exit, not transition to new employment.** In coalfields, dock communities, manufacturing towns, and Appalachia, the primary pathway was not unemployment → retraining → new job, but rather unemployment → incapacity benefits → permanent economic inactivity. Official unemployment statistics systematically undercount the true impact.

**Fifth, income recovery is rare and slow.** Across all cases with longitudinal earnings data, displaced workers' incomes remain **15–25% below** pre-displacement levels even 10–20 years later. Full recovery is the exception, not the norm.

**Sixth, the speed of displacement matters — but not as much as hoped.** Telephone operators benefited from a 57-year national timeline, but incumbent workers at the city level still suffered when cutovers were sudden. Hand-loom weavers' 60-year decline produced prolonged immiseration, not gentle transition. Gradual displacement slightly improves outcomes for future cohorts but offers little comfort to incumbents.

**Seventh, community-level scarring can be permanent.** The evidence from Rust Belt cities, UK coalfields, and deindustrialised port towns shows gaps persisting for **40–70 years** with no convergence trajectory. If AI displacement is geographically concentrated — affecting, say, financial-services centres, government administrative hubs, or call-centre towns — the same pattern should be expected.

**Eighth, children of displaced workers bear measurable costs.** The 9% earnings penalty for children of displaced fathers, the halved university attendance rates in former industrial areas, and the intergenerational transmission of health effects all suggest AI displacement's costs will extend across generations.

**Ninth, institutional responses determine the distribution of pain but not its aggregate magnitude.** The ILWU's M&M Agreement, the NYT's lifetime employment guarantee, the UK's National Dock Labour Scheme, and German Kurzarbeit all cushioned existing workers — but none prevented massive aggregate job reduction. Institutions shape *who* bears the cost and *when*, but the displacement proceeds regardless.

**Tenth, the only intervention with strong evidence of effectiveness is prevention of displacement, not post-hoc remediation.** Kurzarbeit saved an estimated 400,000 jobs at modest cost. Every evaluated retraining programme shows far weaker returns. If AI displacement cannot be prevented — because unlike a demand shock, it represents permanent capability substitution — then the historical record offers no proven large-scale remedy for the workers it displaces.

The uncomfortable synthesis is this: across two centuries, technological displacement has consistently produced a generation of workers who bear permanent costs — reduced incomes, worse health, shorter lives, diminished prospects for their children — while the aggregate economy eventually adjusts through generational replacement. The benefits of new technology are real but accrue to different people, in different places, over different timeframes than the costs. The historical record does not support the reassuring narrative that displaced workers "find new jobs." Some do. Most find worse jobs, or no jobs, or disability benefits, or early death. That is what the evidence shows.
