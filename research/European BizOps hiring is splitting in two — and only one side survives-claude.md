# European BizOps hiring is splitting in two — and only one side survives

**Operations in European tech is not declining — it is bifurcating.** The headline 27% hiring rate and 20% YoY drop masks a structural divergence: routine operations roles (office management, basic reporting, procurement admin) face 65–95% automation exposure and rapid displacement, while strategic operations roles (Chief of Staff, RevOps, AI governance, regulatory compliance) face growing demand driven by EU regulation and organizational complexity. This isn't a cyclical downturn to wait out. It's a permanent reorganization of what "operations" means in European tech, with a 73% collapse in entry-level hiring threatening the leadership pipeline for the next decade.

The convergence of five major EU regulations (NIS2, EU AI Act, EAA, DORA, CSRD) creates an estimated **7–15 new compliance FTEs per 500-employee company** — a counterweight to automation that makes European operations hiring structurally different from the US. RevOps is emerging as BizOps' specialized successor for revenue functions, the Chief of Staff role continues to professionalize, and the entry-level pipeline collapse mirrors investment banking's analyst-class shrinkage of 2015–2016 — a decision that caused persistent mid-level talent shortages for years.

---

## 1. The bifurcation is real: two labor markets wearing one job title

The evidence for operations splitting into two distinct occupation categories is overwhelming and comes from independent sources converging on the same pattern. The **World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025** (surveying 1,000+ employers across 55 economies) projects clerical and administrative workers will see the largest absolute employment decline by 2030. The **Anthropic Economic Index** (March 2026) finds that AI can theoretically automate ~67% of data entry tasks and ranks customer service and office administration among the most exposed occupations. **Goldman Sachs Research** estimates 46% of administrative work tasks are automatable. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects **539,200 fewer office and administrative support jobs** by 2030, with data entry declining 25.9%, receptionists 26.3%, and bookkeeping roles shedding 94,300 positions.

On the strategic side, the picture reverses completely. **RevOps VP titles have grown 300%** in 18 months. AI governance roles didn't exist three years ago and are now mandated by EU law. Management positions face only **3–9% automation risk** according to Bloomberg research. McKinsey finds that organizations with C-suite oversight of AI governance are **2.6× more likely to report material EBITDA uplift** — making strategic ops a profit center, not a cost center.

The academic framework for this split is well-established. Autor and Dorn's **Routine-Biased Technological Change** theory (2013) predicts exactly this pattern: technology disproportionately substitutes routine tasks while complementing cognitive and strategic ones, "hollowing out the middle." Acemoglu and Restrepo's **displacement vs. reinstatement framework** (2019) adds nuance — automation displaces routine tasks but simultaneously creates new tasks requiring human judgment. The WEF explicitly models a "bifurcated" labor market scenario in its 2025 report, where "displacement hits primarily routine roles, while the value of skilled trades and manual occupations increases."

The practical taxonomy for operations sub-roles breaks down cleanly:

| Category A: Routine Ops (Displacement) | Category B: Strategic Ops (Augmentation) |
|---|---|
| Data entry (95% automation risk) | RevOps Manager/VP (+300% title growth) |
| Basic reporting/dashboards | Chief of Staff (growing, professionalizing) |
| Expense processing (85–90% automatable) | AI Governance Officer (new, regulation-driven) |
| Scheduling/coordination (75–85%) | Regulatory Compliance Lead (growing) |
| Office management (70–80%) | Data Governance Officer (growing) |
| Procurement admin (60–75%) | Strategic Planning/COO (3–9% risk) |
| Manual compliance checklists (65–75%) | Third-party risk management (new) |

One important caveat: the Anthropic Economic Index notes that actual AI adoption currently covers only **~33% of theoretically automatable tasks**, meaning displacement potential remains significantly unrealized. Robert Half's 2026 Salary Guide reports US administrative postings were actually up 9% in 2025, suggesting some roles are transforming rather than disappearing outright. The bifurcation may be better understood as a continuum — many roles contain both automatable components (compliance checklists) and judgment-intensive components (interpreting regulatory requirements) — but the directional split is unmistakable.

## 2. EU compliance creates an operations jobs engine with no US equivalent

The EU's regulatory surge in 2024–2025 creates a structural demand floor for operations hiring that has no parallel in the US market. Five major regulations — NIS2, EU AI Act, European Accessibility Act, DORA, and CSRD — collectively require an estimated **7.5–15.5 new FTEs for a 500-employee company, 16–28 FTEs at 1,000 employees, and 28–55 FTEs at 5,000 employees**. These figures are derived from ENISA surveys (1,350 organizations), Deloitte readiness assessments, EFRAG cost studies, and EU Commission impact assessments.

**NIS2 creates the broadest operational burden.** Affecting 160,000+ entities across the EU, it requires cybersecurity operations managers, incident response coordinators (with a 24-hour early warning obligation), supply chain security auditors, and mandatory management training. ENISA's 2024 survey found **89% of organizations need additional cybersecurity staff**, with a median of 2 and average of 4 planned FTE hires. At market scale, this translates to **320,000–640,000 new cybersecurity positions** EU-wide — though 32% of organizations (59% of SMEs) report struggling to fill these roles.

**DORA imposes the highest per-company burden** among financial services firms. Deloitte's 2025 readiness survey found companies dedicate an average of **5–8 FTEs** to DORA compliance, with 64% spending €2–5 million on their programs. Only 8% reported full compliance with the resilience testing and third-party risk management requirements, suggesting hiring pressure will intensify through 2026–2027.

**The EU AI Act creates an entirely new job category.** With high-risk system compliance requirements enforceable from August 2, 2026, companies deploying AI systems need AI Compliance Officers, risk assessment specialists, technical documentation leads, human oversight coordinators, and conformity assessment professionals. The role of AI Officer/Chief AI Officer mirrors GDPR's creation of the Data Protection Officer — expected to become a "hallmark of strong corporate governance." For AI-intensive companies, this regulation alone could require **3–12 FTEs** depending on the number of high-risk systems deployed. Non-compliance fines reach **€35–40 million or 7% of global turnover**, creating existential incentives to hire.

**CSRD was dramatically narrowed** by the Omnibus I simplification package in December 2025, restricting scope to companies with 1,000+ employees and €450M+ turnover — removing roughly 90% of previously in-scope companies. However, EFRAG estimates remaining in-scope companies face **€320K in annual recurring costs** (approximately 2–2.5 FTEs for internal costs alone). Notably, an osapiens survey found 90% of companies removed from scope plan to maintain or expand reporting anyway.

**The EAA creates the lowest standalone burden** (1–6 FTEs depending on company size), as much accessibility work embeds into existing development workflows. But enforcement is already active — France issued legal notices to major retailers within days of the June 2025 deadline, and penalties reach €100,000 per violation in Germany and up to 5% of annual revenue in Italy.

Two critical caveats deserve emphasis. First, companies subject to multiple regulations can achieve **20–30% efficiency** through shared compliance infrastructure — a combined cybersecurity/resilience team handling both NIS2 and DORA, for example. Second, these estimates come partly from consulting firm and vendor sources that may overstate burden to drive business; official sources (ENISA, EFRAG, EU Commission) were prioritized where available. Data on EU AI Act staffing impact is particularly thin, as the regulation is still being phased in.

## 3. RevOps is not eating BizOps — it's specializing out of it

RevOps adoption has exploded globally — **79% of organizations** now have a formal RevOps function, up from 33% in 2020, and Gartner's prediction that 75% of highest-growth companies would deploy RevOps by 2025 appears to have been met. VP of Revenue Operations titles grew 300% in 18 months. Companies with dedicated RevOps report **36% more revenue growth** and 28% higher profitability per Forrester. The RevOps platform market, valued at $3.65 billion in 2023, is projected to reach $18 billion by 2033.

But **Europe lags the US by an estimated 3–5 years** in formal RevOps adoption. A Paris-based Sales Ops Lead at Livestorm noted in the RevOps Co-op community: "The term RevOps has existed in the US for more than a decade, but in Europe and particularly in France, it's only in the last few years the title has begun to be defined." RevOps did not appear in LinkedIn's European "Jobs on the Rise" top 25 in 2023 or 2025, though related GTM operations roles were prominent. European job boards show roughly 235 remote RevOps postings across the continent, with average salaries of **€74,186** (Europe-wide) and **£81,502** (UK) — approximately 40–60% of US equivalents in raw terms.

The relationship between RevOps and BizOps is specialization, not replacement. RevOps owns the go-to-market engine — sales, marketing, and customer success alignment, pipeline management, CRM, revenue forecasting. BizOps remains "the connective tissue of the entire company," spanning HR, IT, finance, product, and broader strategic operations. As one RevOps consultancy framed it: "If RevOps is the engine of the car, BizOps is the chassis, the transmission, and the dashboard." LinkedIn still shows 1,100+ open BizOps roles weekly, and 73% of BizOps teams own both process optimization and strategic planning.

There is genuine title migration occurring. In the Weflow/RevOps Co-op Salary Report, 72% of respondents had "RevOps" or "Revenue Operations" in their title, but 14% used "Sales Operations" and a significant share used "Business Operations" — suggesting some relabeling. In European startups, where BizOps was never as dominant a title as in US Silicon Valley (Europeans more commonly used "Strategy & Operations" or functional ops titles), RevOps is emerging as a **new distinct function** rather than cannibalizing an existing BizOps team.

Key adoption drivers in Europe include CRM platform maturity (HubSpot derives ~40% of revenue from EMEA/APAC), post-2022 efficiency pressure from the funding downturn, and the rise of fractional RevOps-as-a-Service agencies in London and Paris. An emerging wrinkle: Pavilion's July 2025 editorial argued that "GTM Engineers are eating RevOps' lunch," suggesting the operational landscape may fragment further before consolidating. European-specific RevOps data remains thin — no European adoption survey exists, and DACH-specific data is virtually nonexistent.

## 4. Chief of Staff is professionalizing but remains a stepping stone

The Chief of Staff role continues to grow in European tech, particularly at **Series B+ companies** where CEO bandwidth becomes the binding constraint. The Chief of Staff Collective (700+ members, heavily weighted toward UK and Europe) reports growing membership and rising compensation — average base salary reached **£108,000** in 2025, up 6% YoY and 30% since 2022. In Germany, PayScale data shows an average of **€76,377**. Sifted has documented a notable pipeline of former European VCs moving into CoS roles at startups: Beatrice Aliprandi (ex-Lakestar) became CoS at AI startup Praktika; Jan Ebbinghaus (ex-Hedosophia) became CoS at Gitpod.

The role sits in a productive tension between stepping stone and career track. **Median tenure is 18–24 months.** Pave's analysis of 2,220 Chiefs of Staff found that **66% leave the company** after their tenure ends, with COO and VP/Director of Operations as the most common next roles. The modal career pattern remains a 2–3 year stint followed by promotion or departure. But a growing minority — particularly women, who hold **80% of Director-level** and 69% of Executive/CXO-level CoS positions — are treating it as a sustained career path. The role has achieved near gender pay parity at 97 cents on the dollar, making it one of the few executive-adjacent positions where this is the case.

**AI fluency is becoming the key differentiator.** The Chief of Staff Network's 2025 report found that AI-First CoS earn **16% more** than AI-Curious peers. The Network warns: "Chiefs of Staff who cling to task-level work risk being replaced. Those who embrace AI as a core competency — setting up systems, championing adoption, and doubling down on judgment, relationships, and strategic thinking — will thrive." This tracks with the broader bifurcation: the administrative components of CoS work (scheduling, basic analysis, document preparation) are automatable, but the strategic core (cross-functional alignment, board prep, investor communications, organizational design) is not.

Professionalization continues. HBS Executive Education now offers a Chief of Staff Program. Bain Capital Ventures has published a four-archetype framework mapping CoS roles to company stages. But quantitative European hiring data remains a significant gap — neither Ravio nor Figures.hr appears to break out CoS as a distinct role in public benchmarks, and LinkedIn does not track it in European "Jobs on the Rise" reports.

## 5. The 73% entry-level collapse threatens a generation of operations leaders

The entry-level operations hiring collapse is the most consequential finding in the Ravio data, and corroborating evidence suggests it is **mostly structural with a cyclical overlay**. SignalFire's analysis of 650 million professional profiles found a **50% decline** in new role starts by people with less than one year of post-graduate experience at major tech companies and maturing startups between 2019 and 2024. Korn Ferry's 2026 survey of 1,670+ talent leaders found **37% of companies plan to replace entry-level roles with AI**, with **58% specifically targeting operations and back-office positions**. Ravio's own survey found 50% of Reward leaders cite AI automation as the reason for deprioritizing administrative roles — not funding pressure or cyclical caution.

The pipeline risk is acute. Future COOs typically develop through **10–15 years** of progressive operational responsibility, starting with entry-level roles that provide hands-on process understanding, institutional knowledge, cross-functional exposure, and vendor relationship management. Without these entry points, the pipeline breaks. As Renita Käsper, a Global TA and Employer Branding Leader quoted in Ravio's report, warns: "If you don't hire and nurture young talent now, what will your mid-level and leadership positions look like in five years? We're heading towards some very difficult and expensive recruitment to fill that gap."

The strongest historical parallel is **investment banking's analyst-class shrinkage**. Banks that cut analyst cohorts by up to 30% in 2015–2016 faced persistent mid-level talent shortages that took years to resolve and forced expensive lateral hiring. AI tools now handle work that previously required three analysts, but the judgment, relationship, and strategic skills built through that grunt work cannot be taught by AI. The operations parallel is direct: nobody becomes a great COO by reading about expense processing — they become one by doing it, seeing the patterns, and learning where processes break.

The projected five-year consequences are severe:

- **Mid-level operations manager shortage** (~2028–2030) as the "missing generation" of junior hires creates a hollowed-out middle, forcing expensive external recruitment
- **Institutional knowledge erosion** as entry-level employees who learn company culture and processes from the ground up are replaced by AI tools and fractional executives who lack organizational context
- **Diversity pipeline damage**, since entry-level roles have historically been the key diversity intake point for operations functions
- **Succession planning collapse** as companies lack internal candidates with the blend of operational experience and company-specific knowledge needed for senior roles

Companies are responding with **fractional COO services** (a booming market, particularly in the US), **nearshore outsourcing** (over 35% of Western European businesses plan to increase nearshore outsourcing), and AI automation of routine tasks. But none of these solve the development pipeline problem. Fractional COOs are senior talent temporarily deployed, not future leaders being developed. A notable counter-signal: **IBM is tripling Gen Z entry-level hiring** after discovering the limits of AI adoption, and both Klarna and Duolingo have dialed back aggressive AI-replacement strategies.

The assessment is that entry-level ops hiring will not return to pre-2023 norms, but the current 73% decline likely overshoots structural equilibrium. Expect partial recovery to perhaps **40–50% below previous norms** as companies recognize they've cut too deep — but the structural shift in what entry-level operations work looks like is permanent.

## 6. Why operations needs two AI exposure models, not one

Modeling operations as a single occupation category for AI exposure analysis produces misleading results. The data supports splitting it into two categories with fundamentally different dynamics:

**Category A — Routine Operations** has high AI exposure (65–95% of core tasks automatable per Anthropic, OpenAI, and BLS data), net-declining demand (10–36% job losses projected by 2034), and a displacement character. This category includes data entry, basic reporting, scheduling, expense processing, procurement admin, office management, and manual compliance tracking. The appropriate analytical framework is Frey and Osborne's automation probability index and routine-biased technological change theory.

**Category B — Strategic/Regulatory Operations** has moderate AI exposure (20–50% of tasks AI-augmented, but human remains essential), net-growing demand (25–300%+ depending on sub-role), and an augmentation character. This category includes RevOps, Chief of Staff, AI governance, regulatory compliance, data governance, and strategic planning. The appropriate analytical framework is Acemoglu and Restrepo's reinstatement effect and the Felten-Raj-Seamans augmentation index.

Blending these categories produces an average that describes neither reality. A blended "operations" automation risk of ~50% suggests moderate, gradual change. The actual picture is bimodal: half the function faces existential threat while the other half faces a talent shortage. This distinction matters enormously for workforce planning. Companies that cut "operations headcount" by 30% across the board will eliminate strategic roles they desperately need. Companies that understand the bifurcation can reallocate resources from Category A (automate aggressively) to Category B (hire and invest).

The SHRM 2025 Automation/AI Survey arrives at the same conclusion from a different angle: "Business and financial operations ranks the highest in overall exposure to high or very high automation displacement risk" — but this finding obscures that **management positions within operations face only 3–9% risk**. The occupation-level average is analytically useless without the sub-category split.

## Conclusion: the European operations paradox

European operations hiring presents a genuine paradox. The headline numbers show decline (27% hiring rate, down 20% YoY, entry-level cratering 73%). But beneath the surface, **demand for strategic operations talent is intensifying** — driven by regulatory complexity that has no US equivalent, by the professionalization of RevOps and Chief of Staff functions, and by the organizational challenges of AI adoption itself.

The most important insight is that **EU regulation is creating a structural demand floor** for operations talent. An estimated 7–15 compliance FTEs per 500-employee company across NIS2, EU AI Act, EAA, DORA, and CSRD means that even as AI automates routine ops work, regulatory complexity backfills the function with higher-skilled, higher-judgment roles. This makes European operations hiring structurally different from the US, where no comparable regulatory wave exists.

Three novel takeaways emerge. First, companies should model operations as two occupation categories — the blended average is analytically useless and leads to poor workforce planning. Second, the entry-level collapse is the sleeper risk: the 73% decline will produce a mid-level talent shortage by 2028–2030 that will be expensive and difficult to resolve, just as investment banking's analyst-class cuts created persistent talent gaps. Third, the "European compliance tax" that executives complain about is simultaneously an "operations employment subsidy" — creating exactly the kind of judgment-heavy, strategic roles that AI cannot displace. The companies that understand this paradox will build the operations functions that win the next decade.
